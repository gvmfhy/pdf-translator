Another set of openings arises from the situation itself. If you are stuck in a cafe by the pouring rain outside or witness an accident, it is perfectly natural to voice an opinion or respond to someone else's comment. We still remember one of the first exercises in our intelligence training. We had to position ourselves near a painting in an art museum and try to strike up conversations with visitors by commenting on the work. We counted how many conversations lasting more than five minutes we could conduct in an hour. The assumption was that five minutes would be enough to find sufficient common ground with a stranger for the relationship to continue.

Now consider a couple of openings in which you yourself provide the pretext. You can ask a stranger a question or ask for help. Both approaches are fairly straightforward. Your "target's" reaction, however, depends on exactly what you ask and how. Your imagination is the only limit. Con artists and pickpockets have developed an enormous repertoire of tricks for attracting attention and starting conversations. You have probably experienced something like this: a well-dressed man calls out to you, points to a wallet on the sidewalk, and asks whether you dropped it. The act these con artists put on is so convincing that many of us end up spending several minutes talking to them. It can take us a while to realize that we are being conned...

What if the person does not respond to your question, or merely smiles and walks on? That is exactly what will happen if you suddenly spring on a stranger in the middle of the street. In fact, an intelligence officer preparing to make first contact aims to create a situation in which a question or request for help cannot be ignored. As a professional networker, you must choose your conversation-opening questions carefully. The harder they are to ignore, the better.

The same is true of keeping a dialogue going and holding your contact's attention. Listen to the "small talk" at a party and you will notice that every conversation revolves around a couple of hot news stories and a handful of standard topics of general interest. If you keep up with sports, cultural, and political news and know the most widely discussed issues, that should be enough to sustain a conversation with a new contact and steer it in the direction you want. We recommend keeping a mental "list" of popular topics to which you can always redirect the conversation.

---

## How to Ensure the Next Stage of the Relationship

Once you have successfully "sustained" a conversation with a promising contact, your primary goal is to secure another meeting with that person. We hope you will use all your active-listening skills to make the person interested in talking with you. We also expect you to focus on your contact during the conversation rather than on yourself. We assume you have prepared some topics and questions for this first conversation that might interest the other person. If it turns out, for example, that the person supports a particular sports team, try to elicit a comment on the team's performance, at least if the team is doing well. We advise building up an ample store of topics and questions in advance so you can keep the conversation interesting. Before the conversation ends, your task is to work out what you can offer as a way of continuing the relationship that the person will not refuse. One way to build a "bridge" to the next meeting is to ask for some friendly advice or offer something the other person needs. At least at the first meeting, however, this "something" should not entail commitments that would require much of your new acquaintance's time or resources. Professionals call it a "lead" or a "hook," something that gives you a reason to contact the person again. Depending on your interests and capabilities, you should have a collection of such "leads."

One notorious trick used by intelligence services and criminals around the world is the so-called "dented bumper." Someone else's car quite literally collides with yours. The "culprit" apologizes profusely and offers not only to cover all the damage but also to invite you to dinner and give you a gift to erase the unpleasant memory from your mind. The obvious purpose is to create a plausible pretext for spending as much time with you as possible while building a relationship!

We are not, of course, suggesting that you damage someone else's car to start a relationship, but you should have several strategies in reserve. Depending on your "target," you might offer an introduction to an interesting person you know or share information on a subject important to your contact. Even if you do not have that information now, you will certainly find something worthwhile if you search carefully online or ask your friends. People rarely say "no" to such no-strings-attached offers. They may regard one as merely a courteous gesture, but for you it is a solid reason to continue the dialogue.

---

## Follow Up or Lose It!

When the conversation is over, your primary task is to secure the next step. We strongly recommend getting back in touch with your new contact within 24 hours, or at least within 48 hours, of the meeting. We followed a simple rule: no matter what happened, we sent a "reminder" the morning after meeting someone. You need to remind the person of yourself before the memory of your conversation fades, especially if they had other meetings that day. In your call or message, be sure to mention whatever piqued the contact's interest and gives your next interaction a logical and sound basis, such as your promise to help with something.

As a professional networker, you must create a database of your contacts. This is not merely the contact list on your phone, with names, workplaces, and birth dates. You need to be able to record details of your conversations and track the progress of each relationship, as well as enter information about your contacts' backgrounds, interests, and distinctive traits. These details will later help you analyze your relationships and trace the source of any problems that arise. At the end of the book, we will provide a template you can use to create this kind of record. Such a database is invaluable to an intelligence officer. Incidentally, we suggest storing the contact information on a USB drive rather than on your computer, or even printing it and keeping it in a secure place. You never know what might happen to phones or computers these days, or who might obtain these invaluable notes if your account is hacked!

Corporations have long used specialized customer relationship management (CRM) software of varying complexity. If you like technology and gadgets, you can subscribe to some of these products as a service for managing your personal network of contacts for a relatively small fee.

And one more thing! Memories fade quickly; that is simply how human memory works. Within only a few hours, you are likely to forget many details of a conversation. So please make a habit of taking notes while the details are still fresh, preferably on the same day. You must make yourself do this even when you are very tired. Once you form the habit, the routine will no longer be a burden; it will become second nature.

If you think we are asking too much of you, compare this with the system devised by one of the world's greatest networkers, who was regarded as one of the leaders of the "secret world government": David Rockefeller, who died at the age of 101. As Joann Lublin writes in *The Wall Street Journal*, "Mr. Rockefeller recorded the contact information and details of every meeting he had with more than 100,000 people from around the world on 3-by-5-inch index cards. Altogether, he accumulated roughly 200,000 cards, which were loaded into a Rolodex machine custom-built for him. For more than half a century, this five-foot-tall electronic machine stood in his private office at Rockefeller Center in New York."

As the head of Chase Manhattan Bank and a prominent philanthropist, Rockefeller "kept track of a seemingly endless number of ties to the rich and famous, spanning borders, disciplines, and interests, from fellow business titan Bill Gates to the Shah of Iran, President John F. Kennedy, Pope John Paul II, and Neil Armstrong, the first person to walk on the Moon." "Thanks to this unusual record-keeping system, he could resume a conversation with someone he had not seen for years as though their last meeting had been the previous week." "Over lunch at his estate in 2015, Mr. Rockefeller gave Mr. Kissinger copies of the 35 Rolodex cards devoted to him. They contained notes on hundreds of their conversations dating back to their first meeting in 1955," Joann Lublin writes.

"Mr. Rockefeller came to understand the importance of contacts while serving in Army intelligence overseas during World War II," Lublin continues. "My effectiveness depended entirely on my ability to build a network of people with reliable information. I can quickly get to the essence of my past relationship with anyone before seeing them again," Mr. Rockefeller wrote in his 2002 memoirs.

What more can we add to convince you? Fortunately, there is no need today to invent special Rolodex systems for databases like this. Open a page and dictate; the computer will do the rest. But David Rockefeller's remarkable experience once again underscores the central idea of our book: if you work on your "ecosystem of connections" deliberately and systematically, networking will become a powerful tool capable of changing your life.

# Chapter 4. Developing Relationships

## Become a "Gardener" of Human Relationships

So, the first meeting went well. You now know each other. But building a meaningful relationship is a long journey, and many "minefields" lie in wait. As in a desert, you will see mirages from time to time. It may seem that the relationship is about to enter a new stage, only for you to realize that it is still far from its ultimate destination: mutual trust.

In movies, spies use sex, violence, and blackmail to achieve their goals. In real life, an intelligence officer's most powerful weapon is closeness and trust. On screen, spies crack safes to obtain secrets. In reality, other people do it for them: people the officers have befriended, like, and empathize with, and who consider being a friend and helping far more important than guarding someone else's secrets. Even people who sell secrets for money or pass on confidential information for some other reason rely on the intelligence officer's trust and emotional support, because their well-being and perhaps even their life now depend on that officer. They must feel certain that the person who knows more about them than anyone else will protect their secrets, respect them, and care for them. The great successes of every intelligence service have rested on bonds of affection, empathy, support, and friendship between agents and their handlers. This was true both of the famous aristocrats of the "Cambridge Five," who worked for the Soviet Union during World War II, and of Oleg Gordievsky, the head of the KGB First Chief Directorate's London station who spied for MI6. The intelligence officer's art lies in building relationships of trust, confidence, and dependence.

Relationships naturally span a whole spectrum of levels and shades, from casual acquaintances to close friends. When discussing what happens in the Productivity Circle, we noted that a relationship need not be very close to produce results that matter to you. Mutual trust is a delicate thing and difficult to achieve with every contact. For a relationship to be considered stable and productive, however, it must reach a certain level. An intelligence officer, businessperson, or any other networker then becomes a kind of "gardener" who "grows" relationships, protecting them from "winds" and "weeds" in the hope that one day they will bear the desired fruit.

---

## Two Stages in Relationships

The process of building a relationship has two stages. The first lasts from the initial contact until the relationship becomes stable. This means that there is a clear reason for the people to continue interacting personally that is not tied to a particular event or transaction. The participants are not necessarily emotionally close, however. At this stage, you cannot expect the person to do something for you that would require great effort, much less risk, on their part.

The second stage is the deepening of the relationship. The people now truly open up to each other and become "accomplices," in the sense that they trust each other enough to share personal secrets and take business or personal risks together. Clearly, many relationships will never reach this second stage, but the closer they come to mutual trust, the more opportunities they create.

Some people seem simply made to be together, made to be friends. In most friendships, however, as in marriage, success depends on both the effort and the talent of the parties. The problem is that a talent for building relationships, like any other talent, is rarely distributed evenly. This is true even among intelligence officers. Some can "skim the cream" from a large number of contacts while sacrificing depth; others can extract the greatest possible depth and results from fewer connections. There are many cases in which one officer makes contact with a potential recruitment candidate and then hands the person over to a colleague for assessment and cultivation. They are like hunters and gardeners: each has different methods and a different path to success.

Do you know people who seem to know everyone and everything but appear incapable of building deep relationships or speaking with anyone "heart to heart"? Or perhaps you have only a few close friends, but you have known them since childhood and they will remain with you throughout your life. You probably already sense which part of the relationship-building process comes more easily to you and which skills you need to work on.

Do not equate sophisticated "socialites" with people who know how to develop useful connections effectively. Early in our operational career, for example, we envied people who excelled at party "small talk." We thought this was a skill we absolutely had to master to build the right relationships. The ability to carry on conversations "about nothing" is useful in itself, but our later experience showed that its importance should not be overstated. Champions of "small talk" are not necessarily effective at influencing others. If you want to hold another person's attention, it is far more useful to learn to ask good questions: people would rather express their own opinions than listen to yours. Not being the "most talkative" person at a party is no obstacle at all.

The process of deepening a relationship has its own dynamics. You may see a sharp rise in the intensity of your interactions, with numerous calls and meetings every day, but there may also be lulls when nothing happens. Problems and misunderstandings may arise whose causes no one can explain. Some meetings go well and others do not. Being in a relationship is like drifting down a river: you see the next bend only when you reach it. A holistic assessment of the relationship's dynamics is more useful than dwelling on the success or failure of any one conversation. Every meeting has its own context, purpose, and mood. In some cases, your partner may fake their behavior and mislead you in order to create the desired impression. But no one can fake an entire relationship.

---

## Three "Legs" of Relationships

Relationships between people are like a chair: they need at least three "legs" for reliable support. The more "legs" your relationship has, the better, but three is the minimum that ensures its survival. The three "legs" are Common Interests, Emotional Empathy, and Common Social Circle. Some relationships, of course, rest on only two or even one of these three "legs," but they run a much greater risk of breaking. How many stories can you recall among your friends of two different worlds coexisting happily in the manner of "Beauty and the Beast"? Probably very few. How much time do you spend with people with whom you share no activities, interests, or compelling reasons to meet? They may be pleasant to chat with, but what happens next?

A common interest in a relationship implies the possibility of mutually beneficial exchange, whether of material benefits, business ventures, companionship, emotional support, or something else. If the common interest is unclear, the relationship's other two "legs" are often not enough to sustain it over the long term. The people meet less and less often, and their connection gradually fades.

Emotional Empathy is a mutual affection that helps "synchronize" personalities. It involves compatible temperaments and similar views and experiences. Such similarity does not mean being alike, however. For people seeking intellectual or interpersonal stimulation, opposites really can attract and enrich one another.

Common Social Circle can be defined in either a narrow or a broad sense. Both refer to inclusion in a shared environment, whether professional or purely social. In the narrow sense, it includes the active personal contacts in your Productivity Circle with whom you do business or spend your leisure time. In the broader sense, a Common Social Circle is a group of people with the same position in society, whether by income, education, or social status. Practical experience shows that people from socially dissimilar and distant groups maintain stable long-term relationships only through very specific activities or projects, such as hunting trips or visits to the bathhouse. As the old saying goes, "Birds of a feather flock together."

---

## Stabilization and Consolidation of Relationships

At the very beginning, as you establish a relationship, your goal is to bring the person into your Productivity Circle and strengthen the connection so that it does not break at the first obstacle. The key word here is "stability." Early on, the context of a relationship is usually very specific. Imagine that someone agrees to meet you a second time because you promised an introduction to a venture investor. In that case, your contact sees you merely as a route to a particular goal. Even if the startup receives funding, you will simply be a participant in the contact's relationship with the investors. If the person does not receive the money, you will remain, in their eyes, "the person they wasted their time with." Your goal, therefore, is to move this budding relationship out of the specific context of an investment opportunity and into a so-called "neutral" state, in which the relationship is no longer tied to a particular deal, event, or promise. What now unites you depends only on what you both enjoy or do.

In this respect, consider the story of Soviet intelligence officer Richard Sorge, whom the English writer Tom Clancy called "the best spy of all time." Sorge's extraordinarily friendly manner, charisma, and gift for building connections helped him find common ground with even the most vigilant and difficult people. In 1933, after Adolf Hitler came to power in Germany, Sorge went to Japan using the "cover story" that he was a German journalist sympathetic to the Nazi regime. There he met Eugen Ott, the military attache at the German embassy, who later became Nazi Germany's ambassador to Imperial Japan. Over time, Sorge transformed the initially purely professional contact between an ambitious official and an interested, obliging reporter into a deep personal relationship spanning every aspect of life, including an affair with Ott's wife. Ott ultimately came to trust Sorge completely and became his principal source of critical information about the strategies and military plans of Japan and Germany. Sorge's accomplishments earned him a special place among the great intelligence officers.

## "Exchange Currencies" of Relationships

It is time to address another myth about networking: the myth of "true friendship." We have heard it argued that relationships formed for a particular purpose, whether to get ahead in business or satisfy one's own ego, are somehow defective. Supposedly, relationships are either "real" or involve one party manipulating the other. The idea is that a relationship that is not wholly altruistic is actually "fake" and therefore somehow morally suspect.

Let us debunk this myth. Virtually all the connections in your Support Circle are free of material self-interest, unless a family business is involved. That is only natural. Look closely, however, at the relationships in your Productivity Circle, from very close friends to new acquaintances. Regardless of a relationship's depth, both you and the other person still have a particular reason to interact, and that reason is connected with some expected benefit. It may not be the only reason, or even the main reason, for maintaining the relationship, but it is present all the same. When that reason disappears, you will either realize that the person has entered your Support Circle, or your relationship will gradually weaken. Friendships are not divided into "true" and "false." They simply belong to different Functional Circles and are based on different goals.
