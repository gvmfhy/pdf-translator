## Diagnosing Problems in Relationships

Relationships do not develop in a straight line. Any relationship can encounter obstacles. Even with the best intentions, you will not be able to avoid every pitfall or defuse every mine. The radar you used to scan people at your first meeting, the "OIS" sorting principle (the source-language acronym for "Dangerous? Interesting? Difficult?"), should never be switched off. In some cases, the other person will end the relationship; in others, you will stop communicating because there has been no progress and you have found no common ground. To avoid serious problems, we must act like doctors. We need to diagnose problems in relationships honestly as they arise. That is why your "virtual GoPro camera" must always be running, so that you can detect rough spots or flaws in communication in time.

One warning sign is a change in your contact's commitment to developing the relationship. This may take the form of trying to limit interaction by reducing its intensity: repeatedly rescheduling meetings or failing to answer calls or messages. From time to time, you hear excuses and promises to get in touch, but the overall pattern remains the same. We usually hope such problems are temporary, but most often they are not. If you have done nothing to cause it, the other person's behavior means that communicating with you is not a priority for them. Rather than let the problem drag on, we suggest discussing it directly with your contact. You have nothing to lose by asking a sincere question, and the answer may reveal a mistake on your part that you had not recognized before. If so, you may be able to correct it.

Another warning sign is a lack of initiative in your interactions. At a more advanced stage of a relationship, reciprocity can serve as a kind of litmus test. At the outset, some passivity on the other person's part may be understandable. But if the initiative always comes from you and the other person never shows any desire to call first, something has gone wrong. In our operational experience, we had a case that we discussed at length. One of our potentially interesting contacts never took the initiative, and we practically had to drag him to meetings. Yet when we met, he was very talkative and enthusiastic. He clearly enjoyed these conversations, which went on for many hours, but would then vanish without a trace for several weeks. After long debate, we concluded that he was simply bored and was using us to entertain himself. We decided to stop calling him, and we proved to be right: he never contacted us again.

If someone who had shown no desire to develop the relationship suddenly tries to revive it, it is quite likely that there is something about them you do not know. At best, they may want to "sell" you something. For intelligence officers, such sudden interest inevitably arouses suspicion: there is a serious risk that someone has sent this person to you. That is exactly what happened in our case...

Besides these two signs of impending problems in a relationship, you must also monitor the level of emotional involvement and the growth of mutual openness and trust. If, despite all your efforts, someone does not want to open up, your personalities may clash and the person may never come to like you. They may seem polite and even encourage you at every opportunity, but the relationship will still reach a dead end. For example, if an American never invites you home, there is no trace of trust. Europeans, incidentally, may have a valid reason for avoiding this: many apartments in old European cities are simply too small to accommodate guests comfortably.

One way to test whether a relationship has a future is to describe a problem that concerns you and ask for help. As we mentioned earlier, you should ask only for something the person can do without devoting much time or many resources to it. Their reaction will usually show their true character.

It is difficult, then, to judge the "health" of a relationship from any single interaction, whether pleasant or less so. Relationships depend on many factors. They may improve, deteriorate, or stall. Nevertheless, if you monitor their dynamics carefully and watch for warning signs, you will probably be able to revive most troubled relationships.

To track relationship dynamics, you can use the table below. It provides a kind of reading of the relationship's "atmosphere" across four key factors: commitment to developing the relationship and the intensity of interaction; initiative and reciprocity; emotional involvement; and openness and trust. Scores range from 0 to 3, where 0 means absent and 3 means the maximum possible. After each meeting, rate all four factors according to your impressions. Once you have scores from several meetings with the same person, the pattern in the table will let you assess the relationship's development more objectively.

From time to time, perhaps once a month, we recommend opening a bottle of wine or settling down with a cup of tea and conducting a "debriefing." As you analyze the dynamics of your interactions with other people, ask yourself where your relationships currently stand, what problems and opportunities you face, and what your "flight plan" should be for the next period. Try to see clearly what holds each relationship back and what strengthens it. Assess your connections with each key contact: are you comfortable with each other, and are you living up to each other's expectations? Are these relationships even still alive? Ask yourself what you need to do to fix communication problems or, if that does not work, whether you should reduce the time you devote to that person. If conducted regularly, such a debriefing will not take much time and will bring order and clarity to your thinking.

For anyone who views networking as a conveyor belt of business opportunities, resolving relationship problems becomes an issue on an "industrial scale." Just as a military surgeon faced with a flood of gravely wounded patients must decide whom to operate on first and who is, in effect, doomed to die, an intelligence officer, politician, or sales manager must decide which relationships must be strengthened at any cost and which should be abandoned.

The decision to strengthen or end a relationship with one contact can affect your other connections. Every contact is connected to many others. It is almost impossible to track all these interdependencies and think through the consequences of your decisions in advance without visualizing your entire complex web of contacts. This is where your network map again proves useful.

Once again, we return to the main idea behind strategic networking. Communicating with people is probably the most exciting and, at the same time, the most difficult process in the world. Mistakes and failures are inevitable. You will never achieve 100% of your goal. But even reaching 70% through a thoughtful, systematic approach is far better than the maximum 20-30% you can achieve by relying on intuition or luck alone.

# Chapter 6. "Penetration" into Organizations

Economists distinguish between microeconomics, which studies the behavior of individual firms, and macroeconomics, which deals with the functioning of the economy as a whole. Using this distinction, we can similarly divide networking into micro- and macro-networking. Micro-networking focuses on developing connections with a particular person, from the first contact to close friendship. Macro-networking concerns managing connections with an entire organization and strengthening one's position in society. Whether you are an employee working with a client company, a manager appointed to a new department, or someone planning a career in politics, it is extremely important to become an insider in an organization or raise your status within the group with which you work.

Organization is a very broad concept. It encompasses everything from a military unit to a volunteer foreign-language study group. In an organization or any structured group, your relationship with one person cannot be entirely separated from your relationships with everyone else. It may be an exaggeration to say that an organization acts as a single team; nevertheless, it has its own rules of internal interaction, which we can neither ignore nor change.

---

## "Deciphering" Organizations

The chief distinguishing feature among organizations is their degree of hierarchy. In vertical organizations, hierarchy is absolute: a higher position automatically gives you power over every employee below you, as well as better access to information, which flows predominantly from the top. The opposite of a hierarchical organization is a "horizontal" one. In its extreme form, it is a completely unstructured group in which every member is equal. In reality, the structures of nearly all organizations you encounter are mixed: they combine vertical and horizontal elements.

The pattern of relationships within an organization depends on the formal and informal power of the people in it. Formal power consists of the authority conferred by a person's official position, their role in carrying out the organization's tasks, their place in key organizational processes, and their access to resources. Informal power, by contrast, combines a person's will and charisma, competence, physical attractiveness, and, to a substantial degree, the capabilities of their personal network of contacts.

In an organization, three different sets of relationships interact: bureaucratic, power-based, and personal. Bureaucratic relationships reflect the official management structure. For example, someone who officially heads a department can award a bonus or issue a reprimand, thereby compelling subordinates to meet certain requirements. Power relationships are only indirectly tied to such formal structures. They reflect strength of character, experience, personal authority, and social connections: a person's ability to influence others. Many managers, by virtue of their age and experience, "radiate" more power than their position confers. Power relationships also exist between senior and junior team members who are formally at the same level. Personal relationships arise from the human feelings between people in the organization: love and respect, hatred and fear, friendship and competition.

Because we are focused on relationships between people, questions of power and management are crucial. We need to understand how organizations really function, regardless of what their official documents say. That is precisely why we must distinguish the formal power structures and decision-making processes described on paper from the real ones. Official management structures often fail to reflect the real distribution of power within organizations, whether global business corporations or local charitable foundations. For example, a charitable organization may actually be led by the wife of a business magnate, who formally holds the position of deputy director for events, while its official head, a former minister, appears in public only a couple of times a year.

Without understanding its real power dynamics, you cannot work effectively with any organization; you will simply allow people to pull the wool over your eyes. Here is the essence of our advice: assume nothing about an organization's power structure, and treat it as a "black box" until you have studied it carefully.

Behind the formal structures of organizations are tacit power groups that compete for decision-making authority and control over staffing policy and budgets. Their strength rests on the networks of personal connections among group members and on the influence of their leaders. It also depends on the group's ability to unite against others, gain control of information and resources, and use members' personal relationships to the fullest.

It is important to note that power in organizations belongs to groups, not individuals. Mutual trust among group members and their collective control over the organization's vital functions enable them to preserve their common power. A person who tries to control an organization alone, even from the very top, is always at risk. A group of subordinates can unite to curb an "overreaching" boss and wrest more control for itself. Most often, the boss either follows a policy of "divide and rule," setting groups of subordinates against one another, or personally heads the dominant group.

## How to Draw an Organization Map

An organization map can serve as a lifeline, keeping you from drowning in the complexity of its power dynamics. Such a map will differ significantly from the formal organizational chart because it shows not just functional structure but actual power relationships. Its basic graphical elements, people and connections, are similar to those used on your personal network map; however, it should place special emphasis on functional and power groups.

Because of the internal complexity of most businesses and political organizations, representing them as two-dimensional graphs is not easy. Any picture we draw can only be a rough approximation of the multidimensional relationships that exist in reality. As with your personal network, an adequate picture of the organization will not emerge immediately, but only after several unsuccessful attempts to capture and simplify it. Do not despair: without such a picture, understanding what is happening is even harder! So, as before, let us work through the process one step at a time.

**Stage 1**

At the center of a blank sheet of paper, draw a small circle. It will represent the organization's center of power. Later, place several dots or small circles inside it to represent the people who make key decisions. Sometimes it may seem that only one person dominates the organization and "deserves" a place in the central circle. If so, so be it. Around the central circle, draw another circle with a larger radius. It will hold other, less important "bigwigs." Finally, draw an outer circle marking the boundary of the organization; everyone else will be placed within it.

**Stage 2**

From the center of your drawing, extend lines to the outer circle, dividing the organization into key sectors: business units or functional divisions such as finance, marketing, and research and development. Each sector can in turn be divided into its component parts.

**Stage 3**

Use dots or small circles to mark the key figures in the organization according to their positions and functions. For example, place the head of finance in the finance sector; depending on their actual power, however, they may or may not belong in the central circle of "bigwigs." Once you have placed all the key figures on the map, connect them with lines or arrows showing the direction, quality, and intensity of their relationships, just as on your personal network map.

**Stage 4**

Finally, as a last but certainly no less important step, identify the competing power groups in the organization. These often semi-secret alliances can be discerned, at least in part, by carefully analyzing the relationships among key decision-makers. This closeness is indicated not by the direction or intensity of the relationships, but rather by their quality. The most reliable information about who is on whose team will come from your sources who are themselves company insiders. To show which power group each person belongs to, color the people differently or draw colored lines around the groups.

Once you have a map, use it to develop your strategy. As with personal networks, find out which of your contacts are close to the real "bigwigs," and who could become your "connectors," information sources, "gatekeepers," and "bridges."

## How to Pave a Path into an Organization

A thoughtful, strategic approach matters even in a relationship with one individual; when dealing with organizations, such complex nodes of human interconnection, it becomes crucial. Ask yourself what you want from a given organization or company and who can give it to you. Do you want to become an exclusive supplier? Do you want the organization to hire you? Do you ultimately want it to form a strategic alliance with your business? Or perhaps you want it to contribute to your charitable foundation?

Our goal is to offer recommendations that will help you build the necessary relationships within an organization while making fewer mistakes. They may seem intuitive, but putting them into practice properly will require some experience and persistence. Creating a network map that shows who is who in the organization will help you plan your approach carefully. As you progress, the map will help you retain your growing knowledge of key "nodes" and centers of power, enabling you later to chart paths to the right people.

Using the networking terms you already know, you will most likely enter the organization through a "bridge": someone you met at a conference or through a friend of a friend. Your first and very urgent priority, however, is to find a second, independent "bridge" into the company. Not every relationship will work as intended. Your relationship with the person who brought you in may break down too, closing that imaginary "door." Moreover, this person may well want to control your relationships within their organization or even derive some benefit from doing so. If you build stronger relationships with someone else, they will most likely react with jealousy and displeasure. After all, in a certain sense, every other employee in the organization is a competitor to them.

Clearly, finding an additional "bridge" will take time. At first, you simply do not have enough information about the organization. Your second priority, therefore, is to use your initial contact to reach "capacitors": true insiders who know how the organization functions and how power is really distributed. We hope they can give you enough information to help you avoid becoming embroiled in conflicts among competing groups. If you are perceived as a tool of one of them, this will greatly limit your future opportunities.
