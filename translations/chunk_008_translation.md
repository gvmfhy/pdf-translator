Strong relationships are built on an exchange of something valuable. People are afraid to deepen a relationship if they do not understand what you might demand from them in the future. At some point, you need to state your "vision" for the relationship - that is, what you expect from your partner and what you can offer in return. This does not apply to the very first meetings, but do not put the matter off for too long: your acquaintance will want clarity in any event. If they do not share your vision, it is better to find out sooner rather than later.

Your "declaration of vision" does not necessarily have to be put into words. You can convey the message far more effectively through concrete actions. You should constantly look for something your contacts would value and actively offer it to them. When people see that you are bringing something important to them into the relationship, they will reciprocate.

Reciprocity is the *sine qua non*, the necessary condition for any strong relationship. If one party feels taken for granted or regarded as temporary, the relationship is bound to fail. The "exchange currency" can be anything - from enjoyable time together, friendly conversation, access to exclusive clubs, and mentoring, to help with career advancement or access to investment opportunities. You can be confident that a relationship is sound if you know exactly what you and your partner are exchanging and that the exchange benefits you both.

## Three Parameters for Measuring Relationship Dynamics

How can you measure progress in a relationship and monitor its dynamics? Use your network map to track how a person moves from the Development Circle to the Productivity Circle, how the intensity of the relationship grows, and how they build connections with your friends. This will allow you to assess the stability of your connections and the progress you are actually making. Later in the book, we will offer several tools to help you do so. For now, however, we need a little theory. Relationships have several parameters that describe what is happening between you and your acquaintance. Let us consider them in turn.

Leadership and dependence characterize the parties' initiative and roles in an interaction. If you initiate the contact, it is only natural that you will initially find yourself managing the process. Any reversal of roles - for example, when your contact begins to take the initiative by suggesting that you go somewhere together or by calling you first - shows an interest in you and marks progress. Relationships in which initiative and leadership remain asymmetric for a long time usually fail, except when the roles are purely functional or one person naturally dominates by virtue of age and experience, as in a professor-student or employer-employee relationship.

The flow of communication in a relationship is another element that requires close attention. Infrequent communication means that the relationship has an unstable foundation: the participants have little to talk about. More frequent communication covering a wide range of topics - especially when it shows increasingly clear signs of sincerity, spontaneity, and emotion - is compelling evidence of growing closeness. Interaction between spouses within a family provides a good example of communication at its most intensive.

The opposite trend, in which communication becomes less frequent, should concern you: something is going wrong. The exceptions may be close friends or relatives who live far apart. During our intelligence career, we were unable to maintain regular contact with our relatives for almost three decades, but they were ready to welcome us with open arms at any time.

Emotional involvement and the level of mutual trust are also very important parameters of human relationships. As mutual understanding develops, people empathize with and support one another more, show greater emotional openness, and more often exchange views not only on everyday interests but also on moral, religious, and philosophical questions.

On the scale of personal emotional involvement - the best indicator of the depth and quality of human relationships - relationships range from casual acquaintances and blind dates to lifelong friendship or love. As we develop a relationship, we increase our participation and involvement in another person's life. We not only reduce the emotional distance between us but also expand the range of areas in which we interact.

As a rule, the roles of the participants gradually develop and expand as a relationship deepens. Consider a simple example. When you first meet someone, you know exactly what issue brought you together and what role each of you plays. You may be a buyer or seller, an intern or trainer. In a marriage, by contrast, it is difficult to find an area of life that does not require your joint participation. You are simultaneously a relative, a sexual partner, and a business partner...

## "Peaches," "Apples," and "Pomegranates"

The dynamics of relationship development can also vary with the nationality of the person involved. In an era of globalization, national traits are gradually fading within business circles. Nevertheless, although typical behavior in relationships more often reflects social status than nationality, ethnic and national origin still matters. In the course of our work, we encountered three models of relationship development associated with national characteristics. We will call them, somewhat arbitrarily, "peaches," "apples," and "pomegranates."

In the "peach" model, a person easily establishes first contact and readily develops the relationship to the level of a casual acquaintance with no obligations. Considerable time and effort are then required to get the person to open up and to earn their trust. To the "touch," such a person resembles a peach: they are very soft, and their skin and flesh are easy to pierce, but then you encounter something very hard and impenetrable. Most Americans fit this model. They are very friendly and sociable, yet they are also extreme individualists who carefully guard their personal space.

Compared with American "peaches," Russians are more like "pomegranates." They form relationships with new people far less enthusiastically and are reserved and cautious in the early stages. Once the hard Russian "rind" has been pierced, however, almost no corner of their soul will remain closed to you!

The French, like many other Europeans, usually fall somewhere between "peaches" and "pomegranates" - something like "apples." More reserved than Americans at the initial stage, they prefer not to rush until mutual understanding and trust have matured. Nevertheless, some areas - like the seeds of an apple - will remain hidden and difficult to reach.

Of course, the three models in their pure form are a product of our personal experience. A person's character, motives, and background often play the dominant role in how a relationship develops.

## How to Maintain Numerous Connections

The need to devote substantial time and emotional energy to relationships naturally limits the number of very close connections that can be maintained effectively. This becomes obvious as soon as you draw your Functional Circles. An ordinary person may have approximately 100 connections in the Development Circle, where more distant contacts are located, and up to 75 friends and colleagues in the Productivity Circle. Excluding relatives, people rarely have more than three to five very close, intimate friends in the Support Circle. For professionals who work extensively with people - intelligence officers, real estate agents, lawyers, or politicians, for example - the number of connections in the first two circles may be twice as high. Virtual contacts on Facebook or LinkedIn will add hundreds of contacts to your network, but the pattern of your online interaction will remain the same: you will still devote significant attention to only a small number of connections.

When you have a hundred people in your network, maintaining contact with them is not especially difficult. But what happens when a politician has to think about thousands of contacts, almost every one of whom regards the politician as an important connection and therefore expects attention? This brings one of networking's perhaps overused concepts to the fore once again: prioritization. Not all relationships are equal. Their value rises and falls as your life changes, and your objectives change along with it. Strategic networking returns us to the need to see your network from a "bird's-eye view." This means classifying your relationships and compiling several contact lists with different priority levels.

The first list consists of the key relationships on which your career, income, family stability, and most important future plans depend. You owe the people in this group regular personal attention. On average, you should reserve at least one extended meeting per quarter for them - preferably over lunch or dinner - as well as meaningful gifts for their birthdays and when you visit them at home. In addition, maintaining these relationships properly requires at least one planned phone call or electronic message per month, not counting "likes" on their social media posts.

The second list is the complete opposite of the first. It includes people with whom relationships can, to use a less than politically correct term, be "frozen." Little happens in these relationships from day to day, but they are easy to revive when necessary. Here you can use the full power of online social networks. Even so, a couple of phone calls a year to exchange news and offer birthday greetings are still essential to maintaining these relationships.

The third list contains everyone who falls outside the first two: new acquaintances from your studies or good friends to whom you feel indebted. Because these relationships are often still developing, your approach will depend on the stage and purpose of each one. Our experience suggests that stabilizing and strengthening an established relationship requires two or three meetings at which you raise issues of importance to both parties, followed by regular calls and emails addressing the topic you discussed as well as personal matters and interests.

When people meet and a friendship begins to develop, they communicate regularly and often. They grow accustomed to and actively learn about one another. Each forms a mental image of the other and adapts their behavior to the new friend. As mutual understanding grows, the interval between meetings can increase. Properly maintaining a relationship in the development stage may require anywhere from two meetings a month to one every two months, depending on the dynamics of emotional involvement. Relationships that are already stable and sufficiently close can withstand longer breaks without losing their significance.

## Remote Networking

Years of travel and extensive experience moving our "operational base" from one country to another gave us a global network spanning every continent. Maintaining relationships with people on the other side of the world has always been difficult, but modern means of communication have made the task considerably easier in recent years.

We would like to emphasize that networking online and networking in the real world are very similar, whatever anyone may tell you. Online space is simply an extension of your real network, and the approaches we have discussed apply equally well online. Today's popular platforms and tools may change, but the dynamics of relationships among people and communities remain broadly the same. From a purely technical standpoint, building a network on Myspace is not fundamentally different from using platforms such as Instagram or WhatsApp. Let us therefore focus on methods and goals rather than on tools that constantly change.

Over the years, we have noticed that online relationships generally fall into the same three stable groups, depending on their purpose, frequency of interaction, and emotional closeness. These online groups mirror our Functional Circles. The key is to recognize the type of interaction you have with someone online and align your actions with your overall networking strategy.

The most intensive and intimate online interaction is usually "reserved" for members of your Support Circle and takes place almost every day. This circle often contains close-knit groups of friends engaged in an endless conversation, through a group chat, for example. The group is stable because all its members know about one another's daily lives. In this setting, our personal social "filter" is inactive, and we feel that we are part of a shared "zone of trust." This is precisely how deep interpersonal relationships are cultivated online.

The next type of online interaction mirrors what happens within the Productivity Circle. These connections are generally quite purposeful and are sustained by a clearly expressed mutual interest. Examples include book clubs, groups of music fans, automotive forums, and even alumni pages. A common topic helps structure the interaction and ensures that everyone can find something interesting to discuss. A participant's activity may decline over time, but it is always easy for them to return to the group. Such groups rarely sustain extremely close relationships, but they are a good way to establish regular interaction with other members. You may not even know many members of an online group personally, but they can become a valuable and reliable source of new connections and useful information.

One example of the skillful use of subject-specific channels online is the case of Dickson Yeo, a doctoral candidate from Singapore who was apparently recruited by Chinese intelligence. He used his extensive LinkedIn connections to collect information from people with ties to military and government officials in the United States. At trial, Yeo admitted that he had contacted and negotiated with high-ranking officials, using a fake consulting company to post job listings and obtain the information he needed. Yeo maintained a five-year dialogue with one of his many contacts, a U.S. Air Force security specialist, first through LinkedIn and then by phone, periodically requesting various consulting services.

Despite ultimately being detained, Yeo provides a good example of how ubiquitous and effective online networks have become. Intelligence services can take full advantage of online space, not least because they know that, amid the millions of business contacts taking place online every minute, they can recruit new sources secretly and without interference.

The third and least frequent form of online interaction involves contacts you may have "frozen" for a time for one reason or another, whether because of the great distance between you or because there is no immediate need to communicate. These contacts may once have been in your Productivity Circle but gradually moved to its outer edge. You get in touch with them no more than a couple of times a year. We recommend making a list of the people with whom you would like to remain in contact and setting several "reminders" in your calendar so that you reach out on time and remind them of yourself. It is useful to keep notes on what you discussed last time as a way of demonstrating your care and interest in the other person. Fortunately, chat logs make this easier. Interaction at this level usually takes the form of a video or phone call or a long electronic message in which you can discuss important events that have occurred since your last contact.

Before the whole world plunged headlong into the internet, people used greeting cards for this purpose, exchanging them during the holidays and reporting the latest important events in their lives. Our goal is to keep your contacts from "drifting away" for good. They should remain close enough that you would not feel awkward asking them a favor out of the blue. Try speaking openly about personal matters or current problems, and you will be surprised by how readily they reciprocate. To compensate for infrequent communication, you need to give your messages greater "depth" and make them as open and personal as possible, if not "philosophical."

The simplest thing you can do online or by phone is remind your friends that you are aware of important events in their lives, such as their birthdays or their children's birthdays. Modern technology makes significant dates easy to track, but you must pick up the phone or write the message yourself. No one will do it for you. Facebook birthday reminders can be a good first step, but to go a little further, you should remember other important events in the lives of the people around you. This will help you stand out in other people's minds. For a serious networker, every such date is an opportunity to draw people closer. If you diligently record facts about your contacts' family and professional lives, along with the wishes and preferences they share with you, you will accumulate a wealth of useful assets that can be used to create and reinforce a positive attitude toward you.

In the virtual world, the icon bearing your photograph on various sites is the only image by which other people judge you. That is why you should think carefully about creating the right social-media profile. If people find it difficult to discern your human qualities or understand what you like and dislike, or if your profile seems contrived and fake, they will be reluctant to make contact. You will then miss the opportunity to move relationships from the virtual world into the real one.

Online communication is easy to abuse. With a single click, your message is sent to hundreds of people. It may seem original and instructive to you, but your friends may have received the same thing before. We are all inundated with tired jokes, countless photographs of kittens, and copies of email exchanges between people we do not even know. To keep your contacts from unsubscribing or deleting these messages as a waste of time, send your online friends only things they genuinely need and find interesting. Do not forget the wise advice: treat others as you would like them to treat you!

The challenge of personal "marketing" now looms over everyone. One need only look at an "influencer's" online profile to understand how effective personal branding can be. We recommend focusing your efforts on making your profile attractive to the people you want to draw in. Do not try to please everyone. Given the enormous scale of the online world, we strongly recommend a more targeted approach. Once you have established yourself and your image, work on moving relationships from the Development Circle to the Productivity Circle. You will soon discover that your digital life has become a useful extension of your everyday life. It will serve as a kind of multiplier for your usual networking efforts.

# Chapter 5. Methods for Strengthening Relationships

Our discussion of building relationships now moves from general strategic principles to tactics and tools. What can you do to strengthen and deepen connections? As the person with the primary interest in these relationships, you need to take the initiative and determine what should happen, where, and how. You are responsible for the actions that will bring you closer to other people. Below, we offer several methods for strengthening relationships.

The essence of relationship development lies in the gradual removal of barriers between people. These barriers include differences in emotional state, social position, lifestyle, background, political views, and so on. As your connections strengthen and emotional involvement grows, such differences will have less and less effect on the stability of your relationships. If you want a relationship to develop, you need to demonstrate that no differences will affect your friendship. In most cases, you will not be asked to betray your convictions: if you remain true to yourself, people will respect you even more.
