## Synopsis

Deep-cover ("illegal") intelligence officers share the unique personal experience they gained from many years of working with people under deep cover. This book aims to help readers become designers of their own social environment and make effective use of their connections to achieve goals in life and business.

Without setting out to turn readers into spies (although the book may inspire some!), the authors will help you learn to understand people and build productive, long-term relationships. They explain how to manage your own human "ecosystem," including:

- How to strategically build a network of contacts
- How to effectively establish and develop connections
- How to systematically raise your social status
- How to quickly strengthen your position in a new organization
- How to create a circle of dependable friends and perhaps find a life partner

# Preface. Intelligence Is Networking

## Why We Wrote This Book

Networking means building a social network and using connections to achieve success in life and career.

An intelligence officer is a professional networker. Finding new contacts and developing relationships is the officer's primary job, taking up most of each day. That means the work must be done properly and approached strategically, with every step thought through. In work this important and demanding, there is no room to waste time or make mistakes.

In fact, networking is practically the only thing intelligence officers do - apart from cracking safes between sips of vodka martinis, of course, if popular "movie versions" of their craft are to be believed. First, intelligence officers are constantly looking for "sources" - that is, people. Second, they build relationships with people of interest to them, preferably close relationships: close enough that those people are willing to share their most closely held secrets. At the same time, professional intelligence officers continually work on their own development and social advancement so that they can enter the circles where decisions are made. In the process, they build extensive networks of contacts.

Intelligence officers are specially trained to study people. As they gain experience, operatives become highly skilled at practical psychology. They can practically see right through people, and the ability to develop relationships becomes one of their most important skills. They are personable, friendly, and persuasive - just as professional recruiters should be.

Unfortunately, even intelligence officers receive no special training in strategic networking. No such discipline yet exists. Over years of trial and error, each operative gradually develops a personal approach to networking. Many do not even realize that they have a strategy; most do not think about networking in those terms.

We spent nearly a quarter of a century in deep-cover intelligence, working successfully with people of many nationalities and cultural backgrounds. We had many successes, but we also made mistakes. Our deep-cover work, in which we had to pass ourselves off as citizens of other countries, gave us invaluable experience. We built our networks of contacts from scratch in four different countries and in three different languages. We had to build a fourth network back home in Russia, where we returned after a twenty-five-year absence. We came back to a country that had changed beyond recognition in a quarter of a century. We found ourselves there without friends, "at home among strangers," speaking our native language with an accent.

This book is an attempt to share our experience of networking. Although many books have been devoted to the subject, some of them genuinely good, they can all be divided into two groups. The first offers advice and recommendations to help you make contacts more quickly at various events and develop your communication skills. Those skills are certainly useful. But just as a general commanding an army need not be its best marksman, communication skills alone will not necessarily help you build productive, long-term relationships with people, much less teach you to manage your network of contacts strategically.

The second group of networking books consists of collections of stories from the lives of people who have built successful careers as corporate executives or motivational speakers. Stories about how outstanding networkers achieved success can indeed be motivating, but they are not especially useful to people without enormous egos who have not been spoiled by huge offices and crowds of subordinates. Moreover, most of the business realities and social conventions described by the authors of such books are unlikely to exist anywhere outside the United States.

In our book, we wanted to look at networking through the eyes of intelligence professionals. We tried to make the book as close in spirit as possible to an instructional manual. Our aim was to make our experience useful to the widest possible audience.

A true professional in any craft is distinguished by having a system. Such a system goes beyond a collection of best practices and life hacks. It has a philosophy of its own and includes rules that make acquired experience repeatable. For example, if you study a great many books by different authors, you will discover certain rules of plot construction and character development that make novels successful.

Applied systematically and thoughtfully, these rules of networking will enable anyone - even someone who puts them into practice with only average skill - to build a perfectly sound network of contacts. That network, in turn, will help the person achieve their goals in life and business. True talent for networking can be amplified by the systematic process that talented people often lack, just as an uncut diamond gains value when a true master cuts it.

The moment strategy is mentioned, the natural question of goals arises. After all, an intelligence officer does not network for fun. The officer has a whole series of operational objectives. One might be to build a relationship of trust with a particular person, which will require many intermediate steps. Another might be to influence a government agency. In any important undertaking, careful goal-setting plays a key role. Without setting objectives, progress in networking cannot be measured or evaluated. In other words, you will never know whether you have achieved mastery in the field or merely picked the low-hanging fruit.

The idea of writing a book and sharing our networking experience as deep-cover intelligence officers grew out of our desire to help readers use their personal connections to achieve important goals in life. We sincerely hope that you, in turn, will later share your experience and successes with others...

## What We Will Cover

We will begin by defining strategic networking as purposeful, professionally informed social interaction between an individual and the people around them. We will treat networking as a structured, systematic activity. Every system and strategy rests on a theory. To become more effective, people need to understand why they do what they do. "There is nothing more practical than a good theory," as the famous psychologist Kurt Lewin said. That is precisely why we will begin the book with a brief overview of some aspects of the theory of personal social ties that we will need later.

We will explain what personal social networks are and how they function. This will allow you to map your own personal network and analyze it in depth. Once your network of contacts has been diagrammed on paper, you will be able to see clearly where you truly stand among the people around you and understand the strengths and weaknesses of your relationships. You can then plan the steps needed to expand your network, make it more productive, and raise your social status.

Next, we will turn to setting networking objectives and discussing the intricacies of building relationships with people. We will examine the process from the very beginning - from assessing a person you would like to meet and making first contact, through building a relationship and developing mutual understanding, trust, and friendship.

The book is structured to lead you from an understanding of networking's basic principles to practical methods, and then to a strategy for developing relationships with the people around you. We have tried to present the networking planning process as clearly as possible. This should help you set carefully considered objectives and see the progress you make in your daily efforts. The book also includes a series of step-by-step recommendations and templates to help you hone your networking skills.

We will then move up to the next level of networking. If developing a relationship with one person is essentially micro-networking, then interacting with a group of people or an entire organization can be called macro-networking. This level has its own rules, ways around obstacles, pitfalls, and best practices.

The results of your networking inevitably depend on your social status: the higher you are in the social hierarchy, the easier it is to attract people, the greater the "reach" of your network, and the more opportunities you have. Ideally, of course, you want interesting people to come to you rather than having to chase after them! To make that happen, you need to become important, visible, and in demand. In short, you need to become a star!

We will discuss how to raise your social status, advance within organizations, become visible in society, and interact effectively within your social environment. Since more and more communication is now moving into the virtual world, we will also pay attention to your virtual connections and virtual status.

Your success in networking will probably not come at once. Putting it into practice will require effort - sometimes a great deal of it. But our twenty-five years of deep-cover work were not easy either! At least you will not have to worry that your persistent, purposeful, and successful networking efforts will attract the attention of counterintelligence!

# Introduction. Networking from a Spy's Perspective

We human beings are social creatures, constantly interacting with other people. Most of the time, we do not even notice how or why we do it. Visiting relatives, meeting friends, or chatting with a couple at the next table in a cafe - all of this is networking, an integral part of our lives. You could say that, for as long as we live, we are constantly networking.

For some people, however, networking is a source of income - the basis of their livelihood. More precisely, their lives and well-being depend on how well they can make the connections they need. Intelligence officers, politicians, diplomats, journalists, salespeople, and everyone else whose income and career depend directly on communicating with others will agree that networking requires a thoughtful, proactive approach. We would like to focus on networking as a professional activity and a strategic way of managing interpersonal interactions. This calls for a special, systematic approach to your social activity.

Remember the saying? "Whoever does not plan their own victory is planning someone else's." Intelligence operatives are compulsive strategists. Their work constantly requires them to think ahead. Would you really head for a dead drop containing incriminating microfilm, or to a meeting with a valuable agent, without first running through and rehearsing every possible scenario in your head? Carry out an important operation without thorough preparation?! Well, go right ahead! An intelligence officer applies much the same approach almost automatically when building a network of contacts.

Let us begin by dispelling a common myth: contrary to popular belief, strategic networking is not aimed at acquiring a circle of friends. It is about developing a purposeful approach to your relationships with the people around you and achieving life goals by creating and managing those relationships. Networking and friendship are certainly related, but they are nevertheless different. To put it bluntly, close and dependable friends are merely a byproduct that may emerge from networking.

Or it may not. Franklin Delano Roosevelt, for example, was elected president of the United States four times and was perhaps the principal "architect" of the post-World War II world in which we live. Yet he never had close friends. He was known as someone who formed relationships easily and walked away from them just as easily. On the whole, he was calculating and vindictive. That did not, however, prevent him from mastering the art of managing and influencing the people around him. His entire rise to the pinnacle of power was a story of strategic networking.

Strategic networking rests on viewing your place among other people from a "bird's-eye view," so to speak. This allows you to see your relationships with people, as well as all the relationships around you, as a dynamic "ecosystem." Once you have a map of that ecosystem, you can navigate it consciously and manage its development, using your time and resources more thoughtfully and effectively.

A clear illustration of the value of this "view from above" is your daily commute to work and back. There are several ways to get from point A to point B. You can give it little thought and take the usual route - that is, sit stuck in traffic on the main road, wasting an enormous amount of time and fraying your nerves. It is the same in networking: you can spend weeks unsuccessfully trying to get through to the secretary of a very important person who is urgently needed to solve some business problem.
