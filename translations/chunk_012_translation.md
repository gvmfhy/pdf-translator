## Influence in Networked and Virtual Organizations

What should you do if an organization has no center of power? What if it has no structure at all? Increasingly, we see organizations with very shallow formal hierarchies, from high-tech startups to virtual volunteer groups. They function like distributed networks. Yet even there, not everyone is equal. Who should you focus on when developing relationships? How can you identify centers of power in nonhierarchical organizations? How can you increase your influence there? Let us try to answer these questions.

In an ideal hierarchical organization, such as the military, your power is a function of your "vertical height" in the organizational structure. All power is delegated from above. There is only one way to increase your "weight": climb the organizational ladder.

But what if the organization is not merely networked but entirely virtual, for example, an online community in the IT or creative industries? How can you build your influence there? To describe the distribution of power in networks, let us use a metaphor. When you look at the sky on a clear night, the first things you notice are familiar stars, stars that look larger because they are closer to Earth, stars of extraordinary brightness, so-called "shooting" stars that suddenly cross the sky, and, finally, large constellations.

The same principles apply in the virtual world. To wield influence, and therefore a certain amount of power, in a personal network, you must either be well known, belong to someone's inner circle, establish yourself as a remarkable and vibrant personality, do something unusual, or belong to a well-known and respected group. If you want to become more visible and more important in the network, consider how to place yourself in one of these categories.

To become visible and well known, you need to act as a kind of "driver" of interactions and discussions, generate traffic around yourself, and take charge of a node on the communication platform. You need to become a virtual "connector."

If you want to get closer to specific people, you need to offer them something of value. You can find and share information that interests them, take part in their discussions, and help solve their problems. This will give you a reason to contact them directly.

But is it easy to become a "bright star"? If you are not confident in the value of your own ideas, you can become a skilled aggregator of other people's ideas. Become the "king of content," someone others can turn to for useful information. Create a kind of knowledge-exchange platform. People who lack time for detailed, lengthy searches will come straight to you. Better still, build a reputation as a source of "great material." This is precisely the role bloggers play, and their audiences often number in the millions.

Finally, a word about recognizable "constellations." As in real life, try to interact with well-known, interesting people. By participating in their chats and commenting on their posts, you will come to be associated with their circle, attract attention, and gain recognition in the online communities that interest you. In short, you will gain status "by association."

# Chapter 7. Up the Social Ladder

For an intelligence officer, building a network of contacts is not the ultimate goal. It is only the necessary foundation and tool for accomplishing a more important objective. Intelligence officers must continually raise their social status and climb the social ladder. If they fail to do so, their value remains quite limited. People who have access to secrets and take part in important decisions sit at the top of society, whether they are politicians, government officials, or leading scientists. If they do not find you interesting, if you do not belong to the same social strata, if they cannot benefit from your knowledge and contacts, or if they do not find your personality engaging, they will have no desire to deal with you.

---

## Social Capital

Continually climbing the social ladder has another name: accumulating personal social capital. Without delving into social-science terminology, social capital is the goodwill, recognition, and help you receive from people you know in the community around you. The accumulation of positive interactions with those around you gives you the right to the privileges of membership in the community and all its possible benefits, both material and intangible. Put simply, social capital is the "who you know" in the old saying, "It's not what you know, it's who you know."

Networking creates social capital. The amount of social capital, or reputation, you possess is a function of your personal and professional value multiplied by your success in networking. Your place in society is, in essence, your accumulated social capital. In business terms, your social status is your personal "capital," while the social capital you accumulate each year is your "profit." Your networking results are your "income." The stronger your personal network of contacts, the stronger your position in the relevant circles.

The value of strategy in networking becomes most obvious when you consider that your effectiveness, the ratio of the results achieved in strengthening your status to the effort expended, depends on how well you have set your priorities.

For illustration, let us quote American career coach Bonnie Marcus from Forbes magazine: "A strong personal network is like money in the bank. Your network can help you become visible, connect you with influential people, and open doors to new opportunities. Building and developing a network is one of the most powerful things you can do to support your own career growth. Nevertheless, our networking efforts often amount to little more than empty small talk, lack a systematic approach and, consequently, are ineffective. You make friends and connections, but those people are not always able to help you advance your career or, most importantly, put in a good word for you at the right moment." The quotation may seem long, but it shows how important a solid networking strategy is...

## How to Turn Contacts into Social Capital

Methods for building social capital and social status can also be divided into micro and macro approaches. As discussed earlier, the micro approach is aimed at increasing your influence over the people around you, while the macro approach concerns your position in society as a whole. At the micro level, there are three proven ways to increase your social capital by drawing on your network of contacts. We have mentioned some of them before, but they are worth repeating here.

First, draw closer to centers of communication, to "connectors." Whether you are dealing with a company, a local community, or any other organization, try to become a kind of "exchange manager," or universal intermediary, and thus place yourself at the central node of all its relationships. The more central and visible your position, the better. Proactivity and volunteering are keys to success. On the one hand, being at the center of communications will help you "build" the necessary bridges and expand your network's reach. On the other, such a position will give you opportunities to do many good deeds: share information, show care and concern, offer help, and redirect resources. The next step is to become indispensable, someone who knows everyone and helps everyone. The payoff is worth the effort. The respect, gratitude, access to information, and new opportunities you acquire in the process will more than cover all your "operating costs."

Surprisingly, "connector" roles in many groups and organizations remain virtually unfilled. Perhaps this is because most people are too lazy or afraid to take the initiative and assume responsibility for communication flows. It is true that this work takes a great deal of time and effort. When one of us volunteered to revive the overseas alumni network of Harvard's John F. Kennedy School of Government, he immediately realized that no one had ever done so before; at least, the school had no official record of such an effort. If the "connector" role attracted so few people in such a famous institution, what can be said of other groups and communities?

Second, try to create your own "advisory board" and fill it with people who command respect and universal admiration. This need not be a formal group, and your advisors do not have to know one another personally. What matters is that these people can contribute positively to your personal growth or help your career by providing advice and connections.

The term "advisory board" may sound overly "corporate," but the practice of surrounding yourself with experienced, knowledgeable people is universal. Several years ago, after the first lecture in a course taught by one of this book's authors at MGIMO in Moscow, a student approached him and offered to serve as his course assistant. Over the semester, he had several lengthy conversations with her that covered subjects far beyond the lectures; the author shared his experience and effectively served as a coach. At the end of the course, he agreed to advise the student's thesis and later gave her a good recommendation when she applied for a job. Most remarkably, as it later turned out, the young woman had offered her help to professors in almost every course she took. Imagine how much advice, assistance, and how many valuable connections she gained during her years of study! We are confident that she will become a skilled diplomat and an excellent networker.

It is very important to include "rising stars" among your advisors and mentors, people who can pull you along as their own status rises. For an intelligence officer, identifying "rising stars" is one of the most important objectives. Such proactive work pays off handsomely. Consider the example of Richard Moore, head of the British Secret Intelligence Service. After joining MI6, Moore spent several years in Turkey in the early 1990s. There he learned Turkish and became an active fan of the local football club Beşiktaş. Translated from the language of social life into professional terms, this means that he was recruiting members of Istanbul's elite. At that time, Recep Tayyip Erdoğan, now president of Turkey, was just beginning his political career in Istanbul. Those efforts bore fruit many years later, when Moore became the United Kingdom's ambassador to Turkey and one of Erdoğan's most valuable supporters and influential friends. Surely the mutual acquaintances he had made thirty years earlier helped!

Third, get closer to the real leaders, the key figures in a company or community. Your task here is simple: learn from them and discover something new and useful for yourself. After all, to reach their present positions, these people took action and accomplished something, and the results suggest that their actions were right. They most likely understand how the real world and their own organizations work better than others do, and better than you do. They also have access to certain resources. By drawing closer to influential people, you gain the opportunity to learn from them and emulate their approaches and behavior. Ideally, you should strive to make them your mentors in the hope that, one day, you will inherit their connections and status.

The famous Soviet intelligence officer Iosif Grigulevich (1913-1988), using the name Teodoro B. Castro, passed himself off as the illegitimate son of a wealthy Costa Rican coffee producer. In Rome, he founded an import-export company and established extensive personal contacts with local businesspeople, especially those with ties to the Catholic Church. Over time, he managed to become a close friend and business partner of the former president of Costa Rica. As a result, in 1951, "Teodoro B. Castro" was appointed Costa Rican ambassador to Italy and the Vatican, making him a key insider among the upper ranks of the Catholic Church and Italian political circles.

## Riding the "Social Elevators"

When it comes to your position in society as a whole, raising your social status is like riding social elevators. You need to determine which elevators are going up and find a way to jump aboard.

Viewed strategically, the choices we periodically have to make in life can put us on different career trajectories, some higher and others lower. Some paths look more promising from the outset. Being born to wealthy and influential parents clearly makes life and a career easier, just as scoring top marks on Russia's Unified State Exam (EGE) or the American SAT (Scholastic Assessment Test) improves your chances of admission to Moscow State University or Stanford. Even if we cannot control our starting point, however, the later trajectory of our lives depends on the choices we make. These trajectories do not necessarily lead upward. Many intelligent children of famous parents ultimately become failures, alcoholics, or drug addicts. A good education, the right choice of profession, industry and employer, successful friends, and a supportive spouse can radically alter your life's trajectory. At any moment, you may find yourself beside an "elevator" offering short-term and long-term opportunities, and that elevator can carry your life to a higher level. You simply need to seek out these elevators and be ready to jump aboard. Since almost every opportunity for further advancement depends on knowing the right people, your life strategy and networking strategy must operate as a single whole. Your task is to create an "elevator of opportunities" for yourself, as a professional intelligence officer would.

---

## Education - Your "Ticket" Upward

The most obvious, simple, and accessible social elevator is education. Almost any educational program will improve your skills and status. The best programs can become reliable pathways into elite circles. Our intelligence careers included studying in four university programs; the last culminated in a master's degree from the John F. Kennedy School of Government at prestigious Harvard University in the United States. It was a difficult climb to the "summit," considering that we began in an unfamiliar country without even a high school diploma. We had to study at night and work during the day.

The greatest benefit of education, however, is not the new knowledge you gain but the new people you meet. The world of education, including universities, short-term business programs, and professional courses, is society's most open and egalitarian environment. It allows people from different social strata to interact without the social barriers that are inevitably present in everyday life. If you want to build connections beyond your daily circle, you will find no better place.

In addition, people come to study because they are either seeking new knowledge and opportunities or are dissatisfied with themselves and their current work. This means that they are searching for themselves and are open to everything new, including new friends. If you want to fill your network with interesting and successful people, educational institutions have no equal in this respect.

---

## Join High-Status Groups

What else can put you on a better trajectory and raise your status? Membership in high-status groups, of course. Joining private clubs and attending exclusive events are obvious but often expensive choices. We therefore suggest considering other options: professional societies, nonprofit organizations, alumni associations, cigar and wine clubs, sports teams, political parties, and groups that advocate on issues of public importance. Explore opportunities to volunteer, attend think-tank presentations, and help sponsor public events, amateur theater productions, and exhibitions.

Klaus Schwab is a wonderful example of someone who made a name and a career for himself by organizing prestigious events. At age thirty-three, while a professor at a Swiss university, he created a discussion forum now known as the World Economic Forum. Schwab can be considered a peerless networker. He skillfully used his organization to establish connections with presidents and kings around the world. His most remarkable achievement, however, is that he created a networking machine that did this work for him! As the saying goes, hats off to such talent!

While building your career and climbing the corporate ladder, you naturally spend most of your time with people in or around your business and profession. If you want to establish yourself in the upper strata of society, however, you will need to make a special effort to expand your network and build connections outside your professional sphere.

There are periods of rapid career growth, but never rule out stagnation or even decline. Your status in professional circles is closely tied to the status of your employer. If luck turns against you, your colleagues may lose interest in you. The same will happen when you retire. Relationships that developed in other areas of life, however, will most likely endure. After all, they are based less on business and professional interests than on all sorts of other interests.

As an additional benefit, a broad range of connections in different spheres will allow you to become a "connector" for your friends and facilitate exchanges in "relationship currency." Most importantly, you will have something money cannot buy: an interesting and varied life!

---

## Enter the Public Arena

Another way to raise your social status is to become publicly known. You do not have to win the Nobel Prize in Literature or become a senator to hold a prominent place in society. Even average bloggers can amass up to several hundred thousand followers. If you have interesting content, you do not need to own media platforms such as CNN, RT, or Al Jazeera to share it with the rest of the world. Almost any degree of fame gives you leverage in the public arena and raises your status. Once again, content plays a key role! If you have the opportunity, write, make a film, or publish interesting material. If you do not feel capable of creating good content yourself, become a content aggregator or manage a content platform under your own name. The range of possible content is truly limitless, from cooking lessons to international politics.

Any attempt to do something interesting or unusual will inevitably attract other people to you, whether you are crossing a continent by bicycle or writing a book about growing tomatoes. You will get a double benefit. First, you will find yourself in a new community of cycling enthusiasts or tomato growers, giving you access to new, large international networks for communication.

Second, you will find yourself at the center of attention. People will be drawn to you out of natural curiosity. You have probably heard of the attraction that the mysterious world of "bohemia" holds for influential politicians and businesspeople. The best-known example is Mata Hari, a Dutch exotic dancer in Paris during World War I. She was accused of using her connections with high-ranking French officials to spy for Germany. Although Mata Hari's effectiveness as a spy was greatly exaggerated, the attraction that rich and influential people felt toward the young performer was very real.

To underscore the advantage creative people enjoy in networking, we would like to cite the example of the Soviet deep-cover intelligence officer and talented architect Yuri Shevchenko. During the Cold War, he worked in Europe under cover as an artist and achieved remarkable results. For his many years of work, he received Russia's highest state honor, the title of Hero of Russia. For a time, he alone supplied as much as one-third of all classified NATO documents in Soviet intelligence's possession.
