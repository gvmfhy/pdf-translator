Or consider city traffic from above, perhaps through a drone camera. You can easily find routes around the congestion along less crowded streets and get home or to work much faster. Without the whole picture in front of you, it is impossible to see which streets are clear. In the same way, seeing all the connections around you at once may reveal that the important person you are so eager to reach has a close friend who went to school with your spouse. So, to chart the best route to a particular person, you need a MAP of all your contacts.

But before you can devise the best way around the "traffic jams," you still have to choose a direction. Before discussing HOW to make networking more effective, we must clearly understand WHY we need it. Franklin D. Roosevelt, for example, dreamed from childhood of becoming president of the United States, and throughout his life all his efforts to build a network of the right connections focused on that goal. You, too, need to decide what goals you want to achieve and which people can help you reach them. Your first step is to define your "goals" clearly.

As young Soviet intelligence officers just starting work in a new country, we of course knew WHY we needed a network of personal connections. But where were we to begin? We were overwhelmed by the enormity of the task. We made many acquaintances, and every new contact demanded more and more of our time, attention, and care, yet none brought us any closer to anything interesting. Remember the well-known fable "The Swan, the Pike, and the Crayfish"? We felt that our relationships were pulling us in entirely different directions. It was frustrating. We had created a collection of relationships, but could it really be called an effective network that served our purpose? Only by a considerable stretch...

What we really lacked was strategy. Strategy, by definition, is a path to a goal, a course to follow. As soon as you start thinking about that course, many other questions arise. If you want to move from point A to point B, where exactly is point A? Why do you want to reach point B? Are there alternative routes? Which one is better? And by what criteria?

So, to think strategically, you need a method. It provides clarity and directs your efforts. We knew how to find people and develop relationships, but we had not thought about how to bring them together into a network that would work for us and on our behalf. At the beginning of our journey, we were tacticians, not strategists.

Strategic networking is not simply about building a relationship with a particular person. It is about achieving your life and career goals with the help of all the people around you. In a properly constructed network, each connection strengthens the others. Together, they reinforce your position in society as a whole.

Looking back, we realize that our career as deep-cover intelligence officers was a remarkable experiment in networking. As young intelligence officers, we began with no network at all. We arrived in the country of our first assignment with just one suitcase. We went through this process of establishing ourselves from scratch three times, in three countries. Then, after the high-profile spy scandal in the United States and the exchange in 2010, we did it for a fourth time, in Russia. By then, back home, we knew virtually no one except our parents and a few people at the headquarters of Russia's Foreign Intelligence Service with whom we had worked. We had been away working abroad for 25 years. Even our school and university friends would no longer have recognized us.

Our understanding of how strategic networking works grew out of many years of trial and error. With four university degrees and thousands of varied contacts behind us, we were able to step back and view our extensive experience from the outside. Years of hard work helped us develop a conceptual approach to strategic networking. Looking back on our first years in intelligence, we can say with confidence that, armed with the right strategy, we could have achieved in a year or two what took us five full years at the time. We would like to share the power of strategic networking with you.

Before we go any further, let us address some questions people have asked when we have shared our networking experience. Not surprisingly, most concerned motivation and the psychological barriers that prevent people from putting specific plans into action.

The most common problem people cite is the time required to build an effective network of contacts. Amid our constant busyness and shortage of attention, when even our loved ones receive only whatever we can spare between work and household chores, where are we supposed to find time to network properly?

Let us be clear: networking really does take a great deal of time. Building connections can also be emotionally exhausting. But once you accept that your future depends on the people around you and that a good, smoothly functioning network of contacts is important, and once you begin building it the right way, strategically, you will save an enormous amount of time and effort.

Once you set out on the long road toward greater standing in society, know this: the farther you go, the easier and more enjoyable the journey itself will become. As in any sport, whether running, chess, tennis, or golf, the first steps are never easy or pleasant. But with enough persistence, you will inevitably catch your second wind.

Another excuse is supposedly having "no talent" for dealing with people. It can be frustrating to watch a female friend who talks nonstop effortlessly attract a crowd of admirers and sponsors for her business. "Something is wrong with me," immediately flashes through your mind. But you are wrong. Believe us, there will always be people who are smarter, more beautiful, more elegantly dressed, richer, or simply luckier than we are. In reality, however, such people are fairly rare. Most people are much like everyone else, and that includes you and us.

When it comes to networking, we are all self-made. One of our first mentors during intelligence training said, "No one can teach you to be an intelligence officer; it is something you can only learn." The same is true of networking: you acquire experience and skills only through practice. There is no other way.

Frankly, even after many years of operational work, we admit that we are hardly the most gifted at forging interpersonal connections. But experience has taught us how to make the most of the abilities nature gave us, how to listen to the people around us and catch what matters most, how to minimize mistakes, and how to motivate ourselves to move forward despite rejection and failure. So you can learn it, too.

Is strategic networking ethical? Do all these strategies make human relationships somehow unnatural? Are we not manipulating people? If you build relationships according to your "secret" plan, do those relationships not become "fake"?

Such an important question cannot be ignored, so there is no point in postponing it. Every relationship has two sides. It is easier to begin with your own side. When you badly need someone, for example for your business, or simply fall in love with someone, trying to win that person over by every reasonable and honest means is not only entirely justified but perfectly natural.

Romeo's nightly serenades beneath Juliet's window would probably strike people today as excessively extravagant, but hardly anyone would condemn his romantic impulse. Literature, art, and life itself abound in examples of attempts to seduce someone or win their favor. In the overwhelming majority of cases, we have no doubt that the attempts are sincere. What we admire in the heroes of films and books, moreover, is precisely the persistence with which they seek someone's favor.

From there, everything depends on whether this person accepts or rejects you. Whatever happens when you first meet, he or she can always say "no." You may somehow manage to "push your way in" during that first encounter, but you cannot force your way into someone else's life. The more you insist, moreover, the stronger the rejection will be.

Now let us look at networking from the perspective of the person who has caught your interest. When you buy a lottery ticket, you cannot be sure that you will win. Quite the opposite: you are probably skeptical of your chances. Yet you would certainly not refuse the prize if you happened to hit the jackpot. Meeting people is also a kind of lottery. There are people you dream of including among your friends, but you do not know how to make that happen. At the same time, there are people around you whom you would rather not see at all. How we feel about people depends on the value they hold for us, whether emotional, aesthetic, or material. You probably have a few friends whom you met entirely by chance. Grateful to fate for the opportunity, you do not worry much about how it arose.

What matters, therefore, is not how you establish or develop relationships, but what you bring to them and how you treat the person who matters to you. Networking as a discipline teaches you how to meet people and develop relationships, but you decide how and why to use those connections in your life.

One thing networking unquestionably does, however, is make you keep learning from others. As part of your strategy, moreover, you will deliberately seek out people who know more than you and who are better than you. Networking will enrich you in many ways. Your professional value will grow faster. You will develop a better understanding with your loved ones, your colleagues, and your superiors. Once you are accustomed to thinking strategically, you will notice problems earlier and avoid painful conflicts and the loss of friends. And we guarantee that you will become wiser.

# Chapter 1. Networking Tools

As deep-cover intelligence officers, we performed three tasks at once. Our primary task, as you would expect, was intelligence collection. The second was "cover work," meaning our "day-to-day" activity that allowed us to account for our income and interests. The third was to build a network of contacts and improve our social standing, a constant and often exhausting climb from the bottom of society to the top. In fact, all the time that appeared to be ours for leisure, including walks, celebrations, and exercise, had to serve this third task in some way. The work never stopped and required enormous effort and time, but without it we would have been useless as intelligence officers.

Every profession has its own toolkit. Whether you repair household plumbing, design fashion collections, or teach economics at a university, you rely on particular knowledge, skills, and equipment without which you cannot achieve the desired results. Mastering and perfecting such skills usually takes years. Some professions require special abilities, such as an ear for music or a capacity for abstract thought. It is important to note that every profession cultivates a particular mindset and method that are every bit as integral to it as its tools of the trade.

Intelligence officers who spend years honing their operational craft develop an exceptional memory for people and relationships. Just as an artist can reproduce in detail a landscape seen earlier, or a pianist can play several symphonies without consulting the score, an experienced intelligence professional can remember the details of many people's personal histories and of conversations with them for years.

An intelligence officer's chief weapon is their connections. Their essential quality is the ability to handle a multitude of situations and people, to learn, adapt, anticipate, endure, change, and achieve their goals. In short, they must understand and organize the people and things around them. Without initiative and an internal sense of direction, an intelligence officer would immediately be swept into an uncontrollable whirlpool of complications and dangers. The intelligence officer's mindset, that of an organizer and planner of their surroundings, shapes their strategic approach to networking. To apply this strategic approach, identify mistakes, and think systematically, we need to understand what networks of contacts are and how they function.

## "There Is Nothing More Practical Than a Good Theory..."

When we create a network of the people we need around us, we are in effect weaving a social fabric that connects us with the rest of the world. Constant social interaction allows us to protect ourselves, earn money, have children, and push for decisions that affect our society. The connections we form depend on who we are, what we like, the work we do, and the people we live with. We have enemies and friends, bosses and subordinates. These interactions are not random. They truly describe and define our lives. The bonds that connect us are governed by laws and customs, are emotionally charged, and are embedded in the context around us.

We are social beings who survived, evolved, and flourished because we learned to work together despite personal differences and preferences. To survive, we accepted rules and hierarchy, as well as the need for an interdependence that binds us into families, platoons, corporations, countries, and humankind. Our desires, beliefs, and occupations unite us into different groups, from startups and football teams to Masonic lodges. We cannot live without relying on mutual material and emotional support. These sets of stable relationships around us are what we call networks.

Relationships between people can vary greatly in duration, strength, and intensity. They may be short-lived or lifelong. They arise from very different motivations, ranging from the chance to make money quickly to unwavering devotion. Many people have chosen a terrible death rather than renounce their friends, or have given everything they had to others. The people we know shape our thinking and our characters. In some cases, such as for businesspeople or intelligence officers, our value lies precisely in whom we know. For such people, social and interpersonal connections are their working capital in the most literal sense.

## Types of Personal Networks

Let us begin by describing personal networks. We can distinguish several types. They may be formal, defined by various laws and rules or embedded in an organizational structure, as in a military unit. Or they may be informal. Think of a Friday evening when you meet friends at a cafe to drink and unwind as the weekend begins. In the latter case, there is no chain of command, no hierarchy, and no rules at all.

Social networks can be not only local or global, but also real or virtual. Now that we have Facebook, VKontakte, LinkedIn, Telegram, WhatsApp, various forums, and other similar tools, many of us may have more virtual friends than people we have ever met in person.

Networks can differ in their purpose, such as work or leisure. A network, or part of your network, may belong exclusively to you or to you in your capacity as a member of a group, such as your contacts at a client company managed by your team.

Finally, networks can be active or "dormant." Think of the people with whom you graduated from school many years ago and whom you see, at best, once every ten years at a reunion marking the anniversary of your graduation, or of your childhood friends in the hometown you visit from time to time...

## Your Network as Your "Reflection"

Your network will reflect your individuality in many ways. It will depend on whether you are a man or a woman, and it will change with age. For example, ten-year-old girls have more girls than boys in their networks, but by age 16 the opposite may be true.

As we develop relationships, we naturally gravitate toward people with a similar level of wealth and social standing. The same is true of age, race, and ethnicity. In the intelligence world, it is widely known that the success of the Israeli and Chinese intelligence services depends largely on their use of tight-knit ethnic communities around the world.

Just a few decades ago, when the world was not so interconnected and globalized, being born in the same region, and especially in the same town, played a major role in strengthening social ties. Today, however, meeting someone from the same hometown or region means little to most people. We define ourselves much more often by the school or university we attended.

As a rule, the more diverse your network is in the kinds of people you know, their social standing, and their nationalities, the more value it can potentially bring and the more opportunities it can offer. A diverse network significantly increases your social and career mobility and creates a storehouse of useful life experience.

Some factors that shape our networks can be taken as givens. One, for example, is our family background, which plays a dominant role in our interactions, at least until a certain age. Another is our genes, which direct our networking as if from behind the scenes. As in romantic relationships, they shape our preferences without our awareness. They draw us toward people with whom we can form close bonds, even though those people's personalities and views may sometimes seem the complete opposite of ours.

However, as our long career in intelligence has shown, many more of the factors that shape a network are entirely within our power and under our control. They depend on how we see ourselves, what our goals in life are, where and what we study, and how we earn a living. They depend on our conscious decisions, religious practices, hobbies, interests, and the places we choose to live. By managing the factors we control, we can build new connections and deliberately develop our personal social networks.

## A Map of Connections

If we want to develop networks, we first need to understand what they are and what they look like. Otherwise, we will not be able to understand what is happening to them or how to fix problems as they arise. There are different ways to draw a social network, so we need to agree on some rules. Because each of us imagines things differently, we may depict the same social network in different ways. When we first tried to map our networks during our mission, it proved difficult. We made several attempts to put all our contacts on a blank sheet of paper, but at first none of them looked right. The reality of our relationships would not fit the diagram we had in mind. Take a sheet of paper now and try to draw a map of your relationships. You will see for yourself how difficult and frustrating it can be. Our diagram did not begin to make sense until the fourth or fifth attempt. That is precisely why we will offer some recommendations for streamlining and simplifying the process of depicting your network.

Let us cast our minds back to the Age of Discovery, the era of Christopher Columbus and Vasco da Gama. What made those great voyages possible? Toward the end of the 15th century, cartographers invented a coordinate system that allowed every point on Earth to be plotted precisely on a map. Maps had been used since ancient times, but they showed only the outlines of landmasses. Coordinate-based maps accelerated the revolution in navigation. They enabled travelers to set out for distant, unexplored places without the risk of losing their way. The key was to be able to see the sun or stars and to carry a few simple navigational instruments and a clock. Likewise, a map of your network will keep you from losing your way and help you feel secure in your relationships. It will make your network much more productive.

As an example, let us draw a map of a simple network. Imagine a student from Thailand who lives in the United States and also works part-time to supplement her budget. If we place her as a point in the center of the map, the map should have two country sectors: the United States, where she now lives, and her native Thailand. We can divide these country sectors into work, home, and other relevant areas. We then plot her university classmates and professors, her roommates, her coworkers, and her relatives. In the U.S. sector, we will have only her university, workplace, and roommate connections, with no family members. Friends, however, will appear in both countries. Step by step, we can make the picture more detailed and more complex. I think you see the idea... Being able to map your surroundings will help you understand some important concepts involved in developing a network.
