## Respect Others' Personal Space and Time

For most of this book, we have been introducing you to networking and teaching you the skills and strategies needed to succeed. Like any honest teacher, however, we believe it is important to pass on not only knowledge but also the wisdom we have gained from our own mistakes. Our discussion will therefore not be complete until we address the ethics of networking and the problems we ourselves have encountered.

Networking is, first and foremost, a social activity. Beyond simply striving to be a good person, you must respect others and consider how your actions affect other people and the wider world.

Everyone has met people who pester others and refuse to take no for an answer. Do not become one of them. For a professional, such behavior is a sure path to failure. As we have already said, intelligence officers should be "gardeners," not "hunters," in their relationships with other people. If you want relationships to flourish, you must respect other people's personal space and privacy. Intruding into someone's private life or disregarding the boundaries they set is one of the fastest ways to destroy a budding relationship at its roots. Worse still, word of your aggressive tactics will damage future relationships with other people, often before you ever meet them and without your knowing it.

You must also remember that people have only a limited amount of attention to devote to you. Everyone has things going on in their life, and in most cases you will not be their first priority. Do not demand too much of your friends and acquaintances. In addition to having limited time for you, people have limits to their emotional and empathic capacity. There is a reason people say, "A problem shared is a problem halved" (or, to put it in more Russian terms, "Two heads are better than one"). Still, do not expect others to bend over backward for you. There is a fine line between positive support and emotional dependence. Respect the fact that, however much most people may want to help you, they have lives of their own and a limited capacity to care for others.

Friendship can be built, but it cannot be imposed. What is most obvious in romantic relationships applies equally to all interpersonal relationships. Once you see a clear sign that someone does not want to deepen their relationship with you, you must accept the situation and stop trying to change it. As we have already said, you should always have a virtual GoPro camera with you. If these people eventually change their minds, they will come back to you themselves. As a good networker, you should always have a "Plan B," so that no rejection, however painful, becomes an insurmountable obstacle to achieving your goals in life.

We would also like to remind you of the risks that close relationships entail. It is no secret that the most terrible and bloody feuds often occur within families and among the closest friends. The reason is quite simple: when we trust someone, we give that person an opportunity to hurt us where we are most vulnerable. You must always be mindful of how you treat the people closest to you. As intelligence officers, we staked our lives on trusting our Service, our colleagues, and our compatriots. The same tension exists, although at a less potentially fatal level, in every close relationship, whether in business or in personal life. You must do everything possible to retain other people's trust, and you must never use information shared with you "in confidence" to hurt or threaten others.

The final point we would like to mention is the importance of respecting boundaries. Networking is not a game, and people are not tools. We cannot give you a complete, exhaustive list of what is and is not ethical. You must determine that for yourself and establish your own boundaries. Most importantly, you must be able to recognize those boundaries before you reach them. If you fail to do so, you will find yourself on a slippery slope, justifying one action after another as they become increasingly unethical and self-destructive.

## Don't Try to Manipulate People

As you master networking techniques and gain experience communicating productively with different people, you may be tempted to use your abilities to make others do something that benefits you against their will. Even if you are pursuing the noblest and most righteous goals, there is always a risk of falling into an ethical trap, especially if you use unacceptable means to achieve them. Many highly effective methods of managing interpersonal relationships may prove less than ethical. Con artists, abusive partners, and criminals often resort to such methods. If you ever feel the urge to use them, suppress it. Besides being unethical, these methods will most likely cause you serious harm in the long run. Your objective, after all, is to build healthy, productive relationships strategically, not to twist people's arms through emotional pressure or coercion.

In practice, such methods are most often based on false or misleading information in which an ordinary person would find nothing suspicious. In the field of information warfare, these methods are known as "social engineering." Leaders of religious cults, pimps, and con artists use various psychological techniques to control their victims. Without going into the details of these prohibited methods, we will point out one characteristic feature: they exploit the stronger emotional response to receiving help than to experiencing pain, even when both come from the same person.

An abusive partner will first inflict harm and emotional anguish on a victim, then reverse course and offer emotional support. Although this emotional "roller coaster" is a source of suffering and misery, it is a highly effective means of creating emotional dependence and making victims want to prove themselves worthy of their abuser. Under no circumstances should you use such methods, or anything similar, to grow closer to people, much less to control them. Instead of exploiting other people's weaknesses, you should focus on their strengths and build close relationships on a positive foundation. Fear may seem like an effective tool, but its effects are short-lived, and it is ultimately a double-edged sword. In intelligence, fear is the very last resort, and one that can be used only once.

## Learn to Forget and Forgive

Achieving your goals is important, but not at any cost. You are building your network to improve the quality of your life, not to prove something to someone else. Nor are you doing it simply to expand your network without end or chase some high-profile contact. As professionals, we have often had occasion to reflect on the purpose of networking. Early in our careers, an official came onto our radar whose capabilities and connections quite literally made our mouths water as intelligence officers. We were only "one handshake" away from this person and, as the rules required, prepared a detailed plan for approaching the official for our leadership at the "Center." The response from the "Center" quite literally shocked us. "Leave him alone," the coded cable read. "You are not yet capable of handling a contact like that." Disappointed and upset, we tried to argue, but to no avail. A year or two later, during a clandestine meeting with one of our operational supervisors, we raised the subject of the official again. "Look, guys," came the reply. "We track an enormous number of contacts maintained by your colleagues around the world. We see what works and what does not. We keep statistics on contacts, including yours. Even if you had managed to establish this contact, you would not have been able to maintain it. You would have had nothing to offer a person at such a high level. The contact would have withered, and losing it would have eliminated any chance of approaching this person again in the future, once you were ready." In other words, do not bite off more than you can chew...

The wealth of practical experience accumulated by intelligence-service leaders once again reinforces the argument we have developed throughout this book: networking and social status go hand in hand; they are two sides of the same coin. There is no point in accumulating ever more contacts that neither enhance your social standing nor help solve your problems. In the end, the quality of your relationships is what matters. To produce the results your friends and colleagues expect from you, you will have to work at becoming increasingly knowledgeable, resourceful, and appealing.

In short, the better you become, the better your network becomes. As your value to others grows and new opportunities open up to you, increasingly high-level contacts will come within your reach.

Networking, like politics, is "the art of the possible." It concerns what we are realistically capable of doing. Not everything is within our control. Some relationships will inevitably prove difficult. Some people you consider friends will suddenly begin scheming and spreading gossip about you. Someone will ask to borrow money from you and then stop answering your calls. Unfortunately, humanity is still far from perfect. All we can do is keep our own promises and hope that others do the same. Human relationships will always remain something of a lottery - sometimes we win, and sometimes we lose. But take note: if the same relationship problems arise again and again, that is a reason to look at yourself and ask whether you may have overlooked something...

Sometimes it is best simply to turn the page and move on. Preserving the health of your ecosystem of contacts is far more important than trying to retain particular relationships at any cost, even the most prestigious ones. No one is indispensable. Do not take slights too personally. Do not cling to troubled relationships. Never seek revenge. Do not waste your time and energy - keep moving forward. In strategic networking, good spirits and motivation are your greatest assets. After all, you have more important things to do!

# Conclusion. Networking as Self-Improvement

Perhaps the most important lesson our intelligence work taught us was to be proactive. We had no choice but to take control of everything happening around us, especially our contacts. Our path toward the objective meant that every move and every contact had to have a purpose and a plan. As intelligence officers, we automatically became the designers and builders of our human environment. Time after time, step by step, we surrounded ourselves with the people we needed. Our objectives in working with people could vary, but in every case we used a proven set of methods.

Just like spies, you can manage your own human "ecosystem." You have the power to make networking a purposeful and effective process. There is a clear, proven set of rules for raising your social status. There is, however, one important condition: you must be prepared to make a serious effort.

People care about the quality of the things around them. Where we live and what we keep in our homes matter to us. Our human environment, however, has an incomparably greater effect on the quality of our lives than material possessions do. Our success, and even our happiness, depend on how well the people around us suit our character and our goals. Yet we are often willing to accept our environment just as it happened to develop, without trying to eliminate the problems built into it. We allow the people around us to determine our fate, forgetting that we have tools of our own for influencing that environment.

There is, however, one exception! We take great care in choosing our life partners - our spouses. We dream of a person with a particular appearance and character; we search for that person, build a relationship, love them, and care for them over many years. We would not agree to choose a spouse blindly, because we understand how profoundly that person will affect our life. Even with all this care, however, we sometimes make the wrong choice.

What about all the other people around us? Distracted by everyday minutiae, we forget our closest friends. We allow dissatisfaction and conflict to damage relationships and sometimes eat away at us from within. We are too lazy to make new acquaintances, and we miss excellent opportunities.

We wrote this book to convince our readers that each of them can use strategic networking to build a human "ecosystem" organized according to their own standards. We urge readers to abandon a passive, haphazard, and unconsidered approach to their contacts. Instead, we strongly encourage everyone to invest time and effort in a network composed of people they value and respect.

Try applying the strategic networking techniques described in this book. There is nothing mystical or inexplicable about strengthening your social status: it is a clear, step-by-step process available not only to exceptional networking geniuses such as David Rockefeller, Klaus Schwab, or Richard Sorge. We have traveled this path ourselves several times, and we are confident that you can do it too.

As a proactive, strategic networker, you will have the privilege of deliberately filling your environment with people whom you need and enjoy. You will be able to choose the people with whom you would most like to work, making your life more interesting or your business more successful. As you develop contacts and your "ecosystem" expands, you will gain the freedom to "disconnect" from those who bring you nothing but pain and problems. With a large network of contacts, you will always have a choice about whom to interact with and whom to avoid. You will certainly never have to be bored and alone.

Although networking requires persistence and patience, it invariably results in new discoveries: meeting wonderful new people whom you otherwise would never have encountered; discovering diverse and exciting dimensions of life that you would never have explored without making new friends; and, finally, discovering opportunities that would have remained inaccessible if you had not taken that difficult first step yourself.

Networking, of course, is not an end in itself but merely a means to an end. It is a mechanism for continually improving your life and yourself. As you surround yourself with more and more people who are, in many respects, better, more knowledgeable, more experienced, more skillful, and wealthier than you, you also gain opportunities to learn and enrich yourself in every sense of the word. The better and more accomplished the people around you are, the better you become and the better your life becomes. In conclusion, allow us to paraphrase the medieval Persian philosopher and poet Omar Khayyam and remind you that your life will be shaped by the company you keep.
