Preface. Intelligence Is Networking

Why We Wrote This Book

Networking means building a social network and using connections to achieve success in life and career.

An intelligence officer is a professional networker. Finding new contacts and developing relationships is the officer's primary job, taking up most of each day. That means the work must be done properly and approached strategically, with every step thought through. In work this important and demanding, there is no room to waste time or make mistakes.

In fact, networking is practically the only thing intelligence officers do - apart from cracking safes between sips of vodka martinis, of course, if popular "movie versions" of their craft are to be believed. First, intelligence officers are constantly looking for "sources" - that is, people. Second, they build relationships with people of interest to them, preferably close relationships: close enough that those people are willing to share their most closely held secrets. At the same time, professional intelligence officers continually work on their own development and social advancement so that they can enter the circles where decisions are made. In the process, they build extensive networks of contacts.

Intelligence officers are specially trained to study people. As they gain experience, operatives become highly skilled at practical psychology. They can practically see right through people, and the ability to develop relationships becomes one of their most important skills. They are personable, friendly, and persuasive - just as professional recruiters should be.

Unfortunately, even intelligence officers receive no special training in strategic networking. No such discipline yet exists. Over years of trial and error, each operative gradually develops a personal approach to networking. Many do not even realize that they have a strategy; most do not think about networking in those terms.

We spent nearly a quarter of a century in deep-cover intelligence, working successfully with people of many nationalities and cultural backgrounds. We had many successes, but we also made mistakes. Our deep-cover work, in which we had to pass ourselves off as citizens of other countries, gave us invaluable experience. We built our networks of contacts from scratch in four different countries and in three different languages. We had to build a fourth network back home in Russia, where we returned after a twenty-five-year absence. We came back to a country that had changed beyond recognition in a quarter of a century. We found ourselves there without friends, "at home among strangers," speaking our native language with an accent.

This book is an attempt to share our experience of networking. Although many books have been devoted to the subject, some of them genuinely good, they can all be divided into two groups. The first offers advice and recommendations to help you make contacts more quickly at various events and develop your communication skills. Those skills are certainly useful. But just as a general commanding an army need not be its best marksman, communication skills alone will not necessarily help you build productive, long-term relationships with people, much less teach you to manage your network of contacts strategically.

The second group of networking books consists of collections of stories from the lives of people who have built successful careers as corporate executives or motivational speakers. Stories about how outstanding networkers achieved success can indeed be motivating, but they are not especially useful to people without enormous egos who have not been spoiled by huge offices and crowds of subordinates. Moreover, most of the business realities and social conventions described by the authors of such books are unlikely to exist anywhere outside the United States.

In our book, we wanted to look at networking through the eyes of intelligence professionals. We tried to make the book as close in spirit as possible to an instructional manual. Our aim was to make our experience useful to the widest possible audience.

A true professional in any craft is distinguished by having a system. Such a system goes beyond a collection of best practices and life hacks. It has a philosophy of its own and includes rules that make acquired experience repeatable. For example, if you study a great many books by different authors, you will discover certain rules of plot construction and character development that make novels successful.

Applied systematically and thoughtfully, these rules of networking will enable anyone - even someone who puts them into practice with only average skill - to build a perfectly sound network of contacts. That network, in turn, will help the person achieve their goals in life and business. True talent for networking can be amplified by the systematic process that talented people often lack, just as an uncut diamond gains value when a true master cuts it.

The moment strategy is mentioned, the natural question of goals arises. After all, an intelligence officer does not network for fun. The officer has a whole series of operational objectives. One might be to build a relationship of trust with a particular person, which will require many intermediate steps. Another might be to influence a government agency. In any important undertaking, careful goal-setting plays a key role. Without setting objectives, progress in networking cannot be measured or evaluated. In other words, you will never know whether you have achieved mastery in the field or merely picked the low-hanging fruit.

The idea of writing a book and sharing our networking experience as deep-cover intelligence officers grew out of our desire to help readers use their personal connections to achieve important goals in life. We sincerely hope that you, in turn, will later share your experience and successes with others...

What We Will Cover

We will begin by defining strategic networking as purposeful, professionally informed social interaction between an individual and the people around them. We will treat networking as a structured, systematic activity. Every system and strategy rests on a theory. To become more effective, people need to understand why they do what they do. "There is nothing more practical than a good theory," as the famous psychologist Kurt Lewin said. That is precisely why we will begin the book with a brief overview of some aspects of the theory of personal social ties that we will need later.

We will explain what personal social networks are and how they function. This will allow you to map your own personal network and analyze it in depth. Once your network of contacts has been diagrammed on paper, you will be able to see clearly where you truly stand among the people around you and understand the strengths and weaknesses of your relationships. You can then plan the steps needed to expand your network, make it more productive, and raise your social status.

Next, we will turn to setting networking objectives and discussing the intricacies of building relationships with people. We will examine the process from the very beginning - from assessing a person you would like to meet and making first contact, through building a relationship and developing mutual understanding, trust, and friendship.

The book is structured to lead you from an understanding of networking's basic principles to practical methods, and then to a strategy for developing relationships with the people around you. We have tried to present the networking planning process as clearly as possible. This should help you set carefully considered objectives and see the progress you make in your daily efforts. The book also includes a series of step-by-step recommendations and templates to help you hone your networking skills.

We will then move up to the next level of networking. If developing a relationship with one person is essentially micro-networking, then interacting with a group of people or an entire organization can be called macro-networking. This level has its own rules, ways around obstacles, pitfalls, and best practices.

The results of your networking inevitably depend on your social status: the higher you are in the social hierarchy, the easier it is to attract people, the greater the "reach" of your network, and the more opportunities you have. Ideally, of course, you want interesting people to come to you rather than having to chase after them! To make that happen, you need to become important, visible, and in demand. In short, you need to become a star!

We will discuss how to raise your social status, advance within organizations, become visible in society, and interact effectively within your social environment. Since more and more communication is now moving into the virtual world, we will also pay attention to your virtual connections and virtual status.

Your success in networking will probably not come at once. Putting it into practice will require effort - sometimes a great deal of it. But our twenty-five years of deep-cover work were not easy either! At least you will not have to worry that your persistent, purposeful, and successful networking efforts will attract the attention of counterintelligence!

Introduction. Networking from a Spy's Perspective

We human beings are social creatures, constantly interacting with other people. Most of the time, we do not even notice how or why we do it. Visiting relatives, meeting friends, or chatting with a couple at the next table in a cafe - all of this is networking, an integral part of our lives. You could say that, for as long as we live, we are constantly networking.

For some people, however, networking is a source of income - the basis of their livelihood. More precisely, their lives and well-being depend on how well they can make the connections they need. Intelligence officers, politicians, diplomats, journalists, salespeople, and everyone else whose income and career depend directly on communicating with others will agree that networking requires a thoughtful, proactive approach. We would like to focus on networking as a professional activity and a strategic way of managing interpersonal interactions. This calls for a special, systematic approach to your social activity.

Remember the saying? "Whoever does not plan their own victory is planning someone else's." Intelligence operatives are compulsive strategists. Their work constantly requires them to think ahead. Would you really head for a dead drop containing incriminating microfilm, or to a meeting with a valuable agent, without first running through and rehearsing every possible scenario in your head? Carry out an important operation without thorough preparation?! Well, go right ahead! An intelligence officer applies much the same approach almost automatically when building a network of contacts.

Let us begin by dispelling a common myth: contrary to popular belief, strategic networking is not aimed at acquiring a circle of friends. It is about developing a purposeful approach to your relationships with the people around you and achieving life goals by creating and managing those relationships. Networking and friendship are certainly related, but they are nevertheless different. To put it bluntly, close and dependable friends are merely a byproduct that may emerge from networking.

Or it may not. Franklin Delano Roosevelt, for example, was elected president of the United States four times and was perhaps the principal "architect" of the post-World War II world in which we live. Yet he never had close friends. He was known as someone who formed relationships easily and walked away from them just as easily. On the whole, he was calculating and vindictive. That did not, however, prevent him from mastering the art of managing and influencing the people around him. His entire rise to the pinnacle of power was a story of strategic networking.

Strategic networking rests on viewing your place among other people from a "bird's-eye view," so to speak. This allows you to see your relationships with people, as well as all the relationships around you, as a dynamic "ecosystem." Once you have a map of that ecosystem, you can navigate it consciously and manage its development, using your time and resources more thoughtfully and effectively.

A clear illustration of the value of this "view from above" is your daily commute to work and back. There are several ways to get from point A to point B. You can give it little thought and take the usual route - that is, sit stuck in traffic on the main road, wasting an enormous amount of time and fraying your nerves. It is the same in networking: you can spend weeks unsuccessfully trying to get through to the secretary of a very important person who is urgently needed to solve some business problem.

Or consider city traffic from above, perhaps through a drone camera. You can easily find routes around the congestion along less crowded streets and get home or to work much faster. Without the whole picture in front of you, it is impossible to see which streets are clear. In the same way, seeing all the connections around you at once may reveal that the important person you are so eager to reach has a close friend who went to school with your spouse. So, to chart the best route to a particular person, you need a MAP of all your contacts.

But before you can devise the best way around the "traffic jams," you still have to choose a direction. Before discussing HOW to make networking more effective, we must clearly understand WHY we need it. Franklin D. Roosevelt, for example, dreamed from childhood of becoming president of the United States, and throughout his life all his efforts to build a network of the right connections focused on that goal. You, too, need to decide what goals you want to achieve and which people can help you reach them. Your first step is to define your "goals" clearly.

As young Soviet intelligence officers just starting work in a new country, we of course knew WHY we needed a network of personal connections. But where were we to begin? We were overwhelmed by the enormity of the task. We made many acquaintances, and every new contact demanded more and more of our time, attention, and care, yet none brought us any closer to anything interesting. Remember the well-known fable "The Swan, the Pike, and the Crayfish"? We felt that our relationships were pulling us in entirely different directions. It was frustrating. We had created a collection of relationships, but could it really be called an effective network that served our purpose? Only by a considerable stretch...

What we really lacked was strategy. Strategy, by definition, is a path to a goal, a course to follow. As soon as you start thinking about that course, many other questions arise. If you want to move from point A to point B, where exactly is point A? Why do you want to reach point B? Are there alternative routes? Which one is better? And by what criteria?

So, to think strategically, you need a method. It provides clarity and directs your efforts. We knew how to find people and develop relationships, but we had not thought about how to bring them together into a network that would work for us and on our behalf. At the beginning of our journey, we were tacticians, not strategists.

Strategic networking is not simply about building a relationship with a particular person. It is about achieving your life and career goals with the help of all the people around you. In a properly constructed network, each connection strengthens the others. Together, they reinforce your position in society as a whole.

Looking back, we realize that our career as deep-cover intelligence officers was a remarkable experiment in networking. As young intelligence officers, we began with no network at all. We arrived in the country of our first assignment with just one suitcase. We went through this process of establishing ourselves from scratch three times, in three countries. Then, after the high-profile spy scandal in the United States and the exchange in 2010, we did it for a fourth time, in Russia. By then, back home, we knew virtually no one except our parents and a few people at the headquarters of Russia's Foreign Intelligence Service with whom we had worked. We had been away working abroad for 25 years. Even our school and university friends would no longer have recognized us.

Our understanding of how strategic networking works grew out of many years of trial and error. With four university degrees and thousands of varied contacts behind us, we were able to step back and view our extensive experience from the outside. Years of hard work helped us develop a conceptual approach to strategic networking. Looking back on our first years in intelligence, we can say with confidence that, armed with the right strategy, we could have achieved in a year or two what took us five full years at the time. We would like to share the power of strategic networking with you.

Before we go any further, let us address some questions people have asked when we have shared our networking experience. Not surprisingly, most concerned motivation and the psychological barriers that prevent people from putting specific plans into action.

The most common problem people cite is the time required to build an effective network of contacts. Amid our constant busyness and shortage of attention, when even our loved ones receive only whatever we can spare between work and household chores, where are we supposed to find time to network properly?

Let us be clear: networking really does take a great deal of time. Building connections can also be emotionally exhausting. But once you accept that your future depends on the people around you and that a good, smoothly functioning network of contacts is important, and once you begin building it the right way, strategically, you will save an enormous amount of time and effort.

Once you set out on the long road toward greater standing in society, know this: the farther you go, the easier and more enjoyable the journey itself will become. As in any sport, whether running, chess, tennis, or golf, the first steps are never easy or pleasant. But with enough persistence, you will inevitably catch your second wind.

Another excuse is supposedly having "no talent" for dealing with people. It can be frustrating to watch a female friend who talks nonstop effortlessly attract a crowd of admirers and sponsors for her business. "Something is wrong with me," immediately flashes through your mind. But you are wrong. Believe us, there will always be people who are smarter, more beautiful, more elegantly dressed, richer, or simply luckier than we are. In reality, however, such people are fairly rare. Most people are much like everyone else, and that includes you and us.

When it comes to networking, we are all self-made. One of our first mentors during intelligence training said, "No one can teach you to be an intelligence officer; it is something you can only learn." The same is true of networking: you acquire experience and skills only through practice. There is no other way.

Frankly, even after many years of operational work, we admit that we are hardly the most gifted at forging interpersonal connections. But experience has taught us how to make the most of the abilities nature gave us, how to listen to the people around us and catch what matters most, how to minimize mistakes, and how to motivate ourselves to move forward despite rejection and failure. So you can learn it, too.

Is strategic networking ethical? Do all these strategies make human relationships somehow unnatural? Are we not manipulating people? If you build relationships according to your "secret" plan, do those relationships not become "fake"?

Such an important question cannot be ignored, so there is no point in postponing it. Every relationship has two sides. It is easier to begin with your own side. When you badly need someone, for example for your business, or simply fall in love with someone, trying to win that person over by every reasonable and honest means is not only entirely justified but perfectly natural.

Romeo's nightly serenades beneath Juliet's window would probably strike people today as excessively extravagant, but hardly anyone would condemn his romantic impulse. Literature, art, and life itself abound in examples of attempts to seduce someone or win their favor. In the overwhelming majority of cases, we have no doubt that the attempts are sincere. What we admire in the heroes of films and books, moreover, is precisely the persistence with which they seek someone's favor.

From there, everything depends on whether this person accepts or rejects you. Whatever happens when you first meet, he or she can always say "no." You may somehow manage to "push your way in" during that first encounter, but you cannot force your way into someone else's life. The more you insist, moreover, the stronger the rejection will be.

Now let us look at networking from the perspective of the person who has caught your interest. When you buy a lottery ticket, you cannot be sure that you will win. Quite the opposite: you are probably skeptical of your chances. Yet you would certainly not refuse the prize if you happened to hit the jackpot. Meeting people is also a kind of lottery. There are people you dream of including among your friends, but you do not know how to make that happen. At the same time, there are people around you whom you would rather not see at all. How we feel about people depends on the value they hold for us, whether emotional, aesthetic, or material. You probably have a few friends whom you met entirely by chance. Grateful to fate for the opportunity, you do not worry much about how it arose.

What matters, therefore, is not how you establish or develop relationships, but what you bring to them and how you treat the person who matters to you. Networking as a discipline teaches you how to meet people and develop relationships, but you decide how and why to use those connections in your life.

One thing networking unquestionably does, however, is make you keep learning from others. As part of your strategy, moreover, you will deliberately seek out people who know more than you and who are better than you. Networking will enrich you in many ways. Your professional value will grow faster. You will develop a better understanding with your loved ones, your colleagues, and your superiors. Once you are accustomed to thinking strategically, you will notice problems earlier and avoid painful conflicts and the loss of friends. And we guarantee that you will become wiser.

Chapter 1. Networking Tools

As deep-cover intelligence officers, we performed three tasks at once. Our primary task, as you would expect, was intelligence collection. The second was "cover work," meaning our "day-to-day" activity that allowed us to account for our income and interests. The third was to build a network of contacts and improve our social standing, a constant and often exhausting climb from the bottom of society to the top. In fact, all the time that appeared to be ours for leisure, including walks, celebrations, and exercise, had to serve this third task in some way. The work never stopped and required enormous effort and time, but without it we would have been useless as intelligence officers.

Every profession has its own toolkit. Whether you repair household plumbing, design fashion collections, or teach economics at a university, you rely on particular knowledge, skills, and equipment without which you cannot achieve the desired results. Mastering and perfecting such skills usually takes years. Some professions require special abilities, such as an ear for music or a capacity for abstract thought. It is important to note that every profession cultivates a particular mindset and method that are every bit as integral to it as its tools of the trade.

Intelligence officers who spend years honing their operational craft develop an exceptional memory for people and relationships. Just as an artist can reproduce in detail a landscape seen earlier, or a pianist can play several symphonies without consulting the score, an experienced intelligence professional can remember the details of many people's personal histories and of conversations with them for years.

An intelligence officer's chief weapon is their connections. Their essential quality is the ability to handle a multitude of situations and people, to learn, adapt, anticipate, endure, change, and achieve their goals. In short, they must understand and organize the people and things around them. Without initiative and an internal sense of direction, an intelligence officer would immediately be swept into an uncontrollable whirlpool of complications and dangers. The intelligence officer's mindset, that of an organizer and planner of their surroundings, shapes their strategic approach to networking. To apply this strategic approach, identify mistakes, and think systematically, we need to understand what networks of contacts are and how they function.

"There Is Nothing More Practical Than a Good Theory..."

When we create a network of the people we need around us, we are in effect weaving a social fabric that connects us with the rest of the world. Constant social interaction allows us to protect ourselves, earn money, have children, and push for decisions that affect our society. The connections we form depend on who we are, what we like, the work we do, and the people we live with. We have enemies and friends, bosses and subordinates. These interactions are not random. They truly describe and define our lives. The bonds that connect us are governed by laws and customs, are emotionally charged, and are embedded in the context around us.

We are social beings who survived, evolved, and flourished because we learned to work together despite personal differences and preferences. To survive, we accepted rules and hierarchy, as well as the need for an interdependence that binds us into families, platoons, corporations, countries, and humankind. Our desires, beliefs, and occupations unite us into different groups, from startups and football teams to Masonic lodges. We cannot live without relying on mutual material and emotional support. These sets of stable relationships around us are what we call networks.

Relationships between people can vary greatly in duration, strength, and intensity. They may be short-lived or lifelong. They arise from very different motivations, ranging from the chance to make money quickly to unwavering devotion. Many people have chosen a terrible death rather than renounce their friends, or have given everything they had to others. The people we know shape our thinking and our characters. In some cases, such as for businesspeople or intelligence officers, our value lies precisely in whom we know. For such people, social and interpersonal connections are their working capital in the most literal sense.

Types of Personal Networks

Let us begin by describing personal networks. We can distinguish several types. They may be formal, defined by various laws and rules or embedded in an organizational structure, as in a military unit. Or they may be informal. Think of a Friday evening when you meet friends at a cafe to drink and unwind as the weekend begins. In the latter case, there is no chain of command, no hierarchy, and no rules at all.

Social networks can be not only local or global, but also real or virtual. Now that we have Facebook, VKontakte, LinkedIn, Telegram, WhatsApp, various forums, and other similar tools, many of us may have more virtual friends than people we have ever met in person.

Networks can differ in their purpose, such as work or leisure. A network, or part of your network, may belong exclusively to you or to you in your capacity as a member of a group, such as your contacts at a client company managed by your team.

Finally, networks can be active or "dormant." Think of the people with whom you graduated from school many years ago and whom you see, at best, once every ten years at a reunion marking the anniversary of your graduation, or of your childhood friends in the hometown you visit from time to time...

Your Network as Your "Reflection"

Your network will reflect your individuality in many ways. It will depend on whether you are a man or a woman, and it will change with age. For example, ten-year-old girls have more girls than boys in their networks, but by age 16 the opposite may be true.

As we develop relationships, we naturally gravitate toward people with a similar level of wealth and social standing. The same is true of age, race, and ethnicity. In the intelligence world, it is widely known that the success of the Israeli and Chinese intelligence services depends largely on their use of tight-knit ethnic communities around the world.

Just a few decades ago, when the world was not so interconnected and globalized, being born in the same region, and especially in the same town, played a major role in strengthening social ties. Today, however, meeting someone from the same hometown or region means little to most people. We define ourselves much more often by the school or university we attended.

As a rule, the more diverse your network is in the kinds of people you know, their social standing, and their nationalities, the more value it can potentially bring and the more opportunities it can offer. A diverse network significantly increases your social and career mobility and creates a storehouse of useful life experience.

Some factors that shape our networks can be taken as givens. One, for example, is our family background, which plays a dominant role in our interactions, at least until a certain age. Another is our genes, which direct our networking as if from behind the scenes. As in romantic relationships, they shape our preferences without our awareness. They draw us toward people with whom we can form close bonds, even though those people's personalities and views may sometimes seem the complete opposite of ours.

However, as our long career in intelligence has shown, many more of the factors that shape a network are entirely within our power and under our control. They depend on how we see ourselves, what our goals in life are, where and what we study, and how we earn a living. They depend on our conscious decisions, religious practices, hobbies, interests, and the places we choose to live. By managing the factors we control, we can build new connections and deliberately develop our personal social networks.

A Map of Connections

If we want to develop networks, we first need to understand what they are and what they look like. Otherwise, we will not be able to understand what is happening to them or how to fix problems as they arise. There are different ways to draw a social network, so we need to agree on some rules. Because each of us imagines things differently, we may depict the same social network in different ways. When we first tried to map our networks during our mission, it proved difficult. We made several attempts to put all our contacts on a blank sheet of paper, but at first none of them looked right. The reality of our relationships would not fit the diagram we had in mind. Take a sheet of paper now and try to draw a map of your relationships. You will see for yourself how difficult and frustrating it can be. Our diagram did not begin to make sense until the fourth or fifth attempt. That is precisely why we will offer some recommendations for streamlining and simplifying the process of depicting your network.

Let us cast our minds back to the Age of Discovery, the era of Christopher Columbus and Vasco da Gama. What made those great voyages possible? Toward the end of the 15th century, cartographers invented a coordinate system that allowed every point on Earth to be plotted precisely on a map. Maps had been used since ancient times, but they showed only the outlines of landmasses. Coordinate-based maps accelerated the revolution in navigation. They enabled travelers to set out for distant, unexplored places without the risk of losing their way. The key was to be able to see the sun or stars and to carry a few simple navigational instruments and a clock. Likewise, a map of your network will keep you from losing your way and help you feel secure in your relationships. It will make your network much more productive.

As an example, let us draw a map of a simple network. Imagine a student from Thailand who lives in the United States and also works part-time to supplement her budget. If we place her as a point in the center of the map, the map should have two country sectors: the United States, where she now lives, and her native Thailand. We can divide these country sectors into work, home, and other relevant areas. We then plot her university classmates and professors, her roommates, her coworkers, and her relatives. In the U.S. sector, we will have only her university, workplace, and roommate connections, with no family members. Friends, however, will appear in both countries. Step by step, we can make the picture more detailed and more complex. I think you see the idea... Being able to map your surroundings will help you understand some important concepts involved in developing a network.

Network Density and Its Significance

One important concept is personal network density. It is calculated by dividing the total number of existing connections between people in the network by the number of all potential connections, and it ranges from 0 to 1. Simply put, if your network density is "0," all your contacts are separate and have no connection to one another. Sales professionals generally have low-density networks because they maintain separate relationships with clients who are not connected to one another. If your network density is "1," everyone in it knows everyone else. This is quite typical of a study group or a team on a factory floor.

What can density tell us about a given person's network of contacts? There is no inherently good or bad level of network density. Intelligence officers have to keep the density of their networks fairly low, keeping their information sources as far apart as possible and thereby reducing the risks of leaks and exposure. A low-density network is also ideal for sales professionals. For a department manager in a department store, however, such a network would be cause for concern. It could mean that there is no teamwork in the store and that the manager may not know what is really happening in the department.

From a management perspective, network density is a very important concept. If a manager's network density is very high, they do not have many connections outside their organization. In other words, they are stuck in their own bubble. Such a person should probably ask whether this situation is conducive to career growth. From a management perspective, however, very low density is not ideal either. If you do not have enough connections within your own organization, you may be missing something.

In a stable group, when people live and work together for long periods of time, the density of their network increases. Relationships in the group become closer, warmer. The level of emotional support and mutual commitment grows.

Now that we are familiar with the concept of network density, we can better understand the significance of the connections on our Thai student's network map. Some parts of her network in Thailand are denser than others. The connections on the map show that she does not belong to any particular group at either the university or her workplace. She may feel very lonely and uncomfortable in the United States. When someone is accepted as a full member of a community or group, the feeling of being out of place or an outsider gradually disappears.

Three Levels of Relationships

The next concept concerns the distance and purpose of relationships between you and people in your network.

Imagine yourself at the center of your network, surrounded by three concentric circles. We call them Functional Circles. The first, inner circle usually consists of your closest friends and family members. These are the people you trust most. When the occasion arises, you can, as they say, pour your heart out to them. You can turn to them in both joy and sorrow. They form your Support Circle. Unfortunately, when it comes to everyday concerns or problems with your work and career, people in the Support Circle can hardly help you. That is because you have already made use of what they can offer. All you can do with your Support Circle is maintain the existing relationships so that you can turn to these people again when the need arises.

The next circle is usually more densely "populated." It contains the overwhelming majority of people with whom you deal in one way or another. We call it the Productivity Circle, because almost all your useful interactions, work, and social activity take place there. The Productivity Circle helps you achieve results. You know everyone in the Productivity Circle fairly well and have already formed stable connections with them, but that does not mean they are all particularly close to you.

The good news is that people do not need to become close friends in order to be useful to one another. You cannot, and should not, be on friendly terms with everyone. As the example of Franklin D. Roosevelt shows, this does not prevent someone from achieving even the most ambitious goals. President Roosevelt is not the only outstanding networker who did not form close friendships. Another prominent example is former U.S. President Barack Obama.

You will be surprised by how many wonderful things people you barely know can do for you, especially if they see that the interaction is in both parties' interest and you ask them in the right way. Your objective with the members of the Productivity Circle is to identify the most important relationships and make them strong and productive by drawing the relevant people closer to you. For intelligence officers, the most intensive work of "building" relationships takes place in this circle.

Beyond the outer boundary of your Productivity Circle lies another area, the so-called Development Circle. In essence, this is the "horizon" of your network, where new contacts appear. This area serves as a kind of bridge between your network of contacts and the rest of the world. New people may enter the Development Circle and then disappear if the relationships do not become stable and purposeful. They may return if you make an effort or if external circumstances change. The Development Circle calls for a two-part strategy. On the one hand, you need to fill it with new people, creating a continuous flow of additional contacts into your network. On the other, you must assess the new people appearing on your network's horizon and move those who are of interest into the Productivity Circle.

Three Main Rules of Networking

Let us turn to the basic principles of networking. They are surprisingly simple. In fact, they can be reduced to three simple rules. Imagine communication between people as a flow of electrical signals. You can think of your network as a set of light bulbs connected to one another. These connections form nodes. Some nodes may be small, connecting only a few bulbs. Others are enormous, with hundreds, if not thousands, of connections. The same is true of human connections. Some of us establish many connections with ease. You probably remember encountering extremely sociable people at school or work who always had a wide circle of friends and were always the center of attention. Let us call them "connectors." Their birthday parties were always packed. By nature, these people attract attention; others are drawn to them.

The first rule of networking: spot and seek out "connectors" around you, and build close relationships with them. By getting close to them, you gain access to parts of their networks, including people whom you would otherwise have had no chance of meeting in person.

What is a relationship? In essence, it is a process of exchange. This may be an exchange of goods, emotions, or information. Every conversation is also an exchange; at the very least, it involves giving each other attention. When people offer you something, they expect something in return.

To offer someone something - whatever it may be - you must first have it, whether it is money, information, or something else. Where can you get this "something" to exchange with others? Most often, from members of your network who have access to it, whether it is expert knowledge of the stock market or tickets to the Bolshoi Theater. In this arrangement, you are like a broker who produces nothing but still makes a contribution by facilitating a profitable deal.

In your network - to return to the electrical-circuit analogy - there are people who accumulate a great deal of the "useful stuff" circulating around them. Let us call them "capacitors." They are usually highly respected people. Others constantly turn to them for advice. In organizations, they possess institutional memory. If you find these "capacitors" and skillfully build relationships with them, sooner or later they will be able to share what they have "accumulated." Once you acquire something you can share, you will have the opportunity to trade with people in your network. By using what you have acquired as a kind of currency, you can raise your status or exchange it for what you need. For an intelligence officer, finding such "capacitors" who could become information sources is a vital part of the job.

This gives us the second rule of networking: identify "capacitors" around you and establish close relationships with them.

In our imaginary electrical circuit, some elements can disrupt or amplify the transmission of energy: "breakers" and "conductors." In social networks, these are "gatekeepers" (literally, "doorkeepers") or, conversely, "bridges" - people who can put obstacles in your path or open new doors. For example, depending on the quality of your relationship with her, your boss's secretary may act as either a "gatekeeper" or a "bridge." If your relationship is not very good, her likely response to your request for a meeting will be: "You know, he's so busy right now. Maybe next week..." Conversely, if you have a good relationship and urgently need to see the boss, she will say: "All right, I'll call you right back."

People who give you access to other networks, organizations, or countries can also act as "bridges." Do you have any friends from Brazil, for example? If not, and you suddenly need to fly there, you will most likely find yourself alone in a country that may initially leave you feeling uncertain. But if you have a friend from Brazil - your "bridge," in effect - with a couple of emails or phone calls they can easily mobilize their local connections. You will then come away with the most pleasant and unforgettable memories of your trip!

All this leads us to the third rule of networking: identify the people who serve as "gatekeepers" and "bridges," and build relationships with them so that they work for you rather than against you.

In short, networking depends on having "connectors" and "capacitors" in your network and building relationships with "gatekeepers" and "bridges."

How to Draw a Map of Your Network

Now, armed with theory, it is time to take action and draw a map of your network. American writer Mark Twain once said: "The secret of getting ahead is getting started. The secret of getting started is breaking your complex, overwhelming tasks into small, manageable tasks, and then starting on the first one." The first steps in creating a map of your network will be the most difficult. That is why we want to explain them as simply and clearly as possible. Remember: strategic networking begins with a strategy, and your strategy begins with mapping your current network. Let us proceed in stages, step by step.

Stage 1

Take a large sheet of white paper and place a dot in the center. This dot is you. Then draw two circles around it. Make the first one small and the second one larger, leaving about ten centimeters between the outer circle and the edges of the sheet. These are your Functional Circles. The first, as you will recall, is the Support Circle - your relatives and closest friends. The second is the Productivity Circle, which will contain most of your stable connections. The area beyond it will be your Development Circle - the horizon of your network, where new contacts appear. Draw radii from the center to the edges of the sheet. These lines form sectors - some larger, some smaller - representing areas of your life such as your workplace, clients, school, or leisure activities: the areas from which your relationships arise.

Stage 2

Start plotting your contacts on the map: one person, one dot. If someone is important to you, make the dot larger and bolder. You can use your smartphone's contact list as a database. Decide how many people you want to place on the map. Be reasonable: a smartphone usually contains the phone numbers of hundreds of people, but you do not need to include those whom you barely know and in whom you have no particular interest. Try to label each dot with a name or initials.

Stage 3

Next, draw lines between yourself and other people to reflect the nature of your relationships. To keep the picture legible, mark only important contacts. Use a thick line for a very intense relationship and a dotted line for a sporadic one. You can draw arrows on the lines to show who usually takes the initiative. If the initiative is mutual, the arrows can point in both directions. In the same way - using lines and arrows - you can connect people in the network who know one another.

Once all the connections have been plotted on the map, think about how to indicate their quality. Plus signs can represent positive, dynamic relationships, while minus signs can mark relationships that are causing problems. Finally, use color to highlight groups in your network whose members share a particular interest. And voilà! You are almost done!

Actually, no! Not quite yet... You may find that some sectors or rings are too small to accommodate all the people you would like to "cram" into them, while others are almost empty. Although the basic principles of mapping a network are simple and intuitive, it may take several attempts and many sheets of paper before you finally arrive at a more or less workable version that accurately reflects reality.

Do not worry; we warned you that it would take several attempts! Our suggestion that you draw your network using circles, lines, and dots is only a recommendation. Take your time and try to draw a clear, easy-to-read map. If it works, great! If not, do not despair; find another method that works for you. And remember: your network will change, and you will need to update your contact map from time to time.

Chapter 2. Networking Strategy

We hope you now understand how social networks function. It is time to delve deeper into the subject, explore some important ideas, and introduce you to the most effective methods. Developing a network of contacts is something we do every day, consciously or unconsciously. Although how we do so depends on our personal qualities and way of life, there are certain "standard" skills we need to improve in order to master networking as a whole.

It may seem that the rather specialized networking methods we used as intelligence officers working under deep cover would not apply, for example, to the career of an aspiring actor or financial analyst. In fact, the universal mechanisms of networking can be put to use by anyone, regardless of age, profession, or nationality. Intelligence officers simply tend to act somewhat more decisively and place greater emphasis on achieving a specific operational result.

As soon as you apply the previously described "bird's-eye view" to your contacts, you will almost immediately discover problems and shortcomings in your personal network, such as a slow rate of acquiring new contacts, stalled progress in developing relationships with certain people, or inadequate reach. Reflecting on the various problems we repeatedly encountered in our work as intelligence officers, we concluded that most of them are common to everyone and call for a fairly standard set of actions. If you learn to recognize them, you will eliminate the vast majority of obstacles in your relationships, significantly improving your personal and professional life.

Based on our experience, typical difficulties are as follows:

  • Setting appropriate networking goals
  • Developing a networking strategy
  • Acquiring new contacts
  • Developing productive relationships
  • Resolving problems in relationships
  • Strengthening your influence in groups and organizations
  • Raising your social status among those around you

In subsequent chapters, we'll describe how to overcome all these difficulties.

From Life Goals to Networking Priorities

Many years ago, when we were novice intelligence officers, we arrived in the country of our first foreign assignment. However eager we were to gather useful information and achieve results quickly, we felt an acute need for emotional support. We had neither relatives nor friends around us. Worse still, we had no social status to bolster our self-esteem and make us valuable partners in the eyes of others. Our low - practically nonexistent - social status prevented us from actively forming connections with the kinds of interesting people essential to intelligence work. We also needed to build relationships with the people around us so that we could adapt to and adopt local norms of behavior and customs. In other words, we needed a Support Circle. Over time, we established connections with neighbors, doctors, librarians, store and restaurant owners, local parishioners, and members of the clergy. After several sessions with a manual therapist - who, incidentally, was our age - we struck up a friendship with him. It lasted several years, until we had to move to another city. An interest in singing and music led one of us to join a church choir. As a result, we had the opportunity not only to spend time with pleasant people but also to establish important contacts with the local priest.

As we gained experience and confidence, our networking priorities shifted toward developing an effective "cover" and attaining higher social status. Then, when our two sons were born several years later, we discovered an entirely new chapter in networking: the remarkable world of relationships among parents, on playgrounds and at school. Over the next ten years, a completely different life opened up for us, along with a different network of contacts and different priorities. We formed connections with many people in many countries, shared common interests, and interacted with people from different social strata. We could now look back on important professional achievements, which gave us the confidence to move forward and break into the upper echelons of society.

Finally, toward the end of an intelligence career spanning more than twenty years, when we faced unexpected betrayal and arrest, our networking goals shifted toward new priorities, especially rebuilding our circle of contacts after our whole family returned to Russia. Our path shows that everyone has to rebuild their network of contacts and surround themselves with new people whenever they change and the circumstances of their life change.

Whatever your current networking needs may be, they are determined by your goals in life as well as your immediate priorities. It is impossible to overstate how important setting the right goals is for success. Without clear priorities, your interactions will inevitably become an unpleasant, directionless, aimless frenzy that consumes your time and drains you emotionally, with very little return. Once your goals are set, however, your networking strategy immediately makes sense.

Circle of Goals

A more involved method of setting goals requires revisiting your long-term life plans. To turn your goals successfully into a strategy, you need to answer the question of which specific steps will help you achieve them. Whatever ideas and desires you may have, making them concrete enough to guide your networking is a serious challenge. To make the task easier, we developed a simple diagram and used it whenever we felt the need to pause and reconsider our priorities.

At the center of a sheet of paper, draw three concentric circles. From the center outward, label them "What," "Who/Where," and "How." We recommend dividing the circles into four sectors with radial lines. The sectors will help you focus on different aspects of life and career, such as Finances, Health, Family Life, Interests, or Personal Growth.

In each thematic sector of the innermost circle, write down one to three main goals you have set for yourself, such as "have more friends," "earn more," "become a more interesting person," or "improve my relationship with my daughter."

Think of the people who can help you achieve each goal or on whom its achievement may depend. In the next circle, across from the goal "earn more," write the names of people who could potentially help. Likewise, across from the goal "become a more interesting person," write the names of people you know or would like to know who could contribute to your personal and professional growth. If the names of the right people do not immediately come to mind, write down where you might find such people, such as in a professional association.

In the outer circle, across from each name or place, briefly note how you intend to build relationships with the people you need and who might introduce you to them.

Writing down your problems and setting short-term and long-term goals help organize your thoughts. No matter how precise or detailed your initial answers may be, every time you return to the circle and try to answer the questions, you improve your ability to achieve the goals you have set.

Assess Your Resources

Once you have defined your goals, begin analyzing the people you know. Copy the contact list from your phone and sort people into groups such as family and family friends, former classmates, work colleagues, and people you encounter at sports facilities or other events.

Using your knowledge of networking theory, find the people who can act as "connectors" and "capacitors" in your network of contacts. Identify the "gatekeepers" and the people who can serve as "bridges," opening a path to other people or organizations. This will help you prioritize your contacts by their usefulness going forward and identify those who could potentially introduce you to other people you need.

If you feel that your main networking problem is forming new contacts, give top priority to building relationships with "connectors." By gaining access to some of their contacts, you will also gain new opportunities. Besides people who act as "connectors" because of certain personal qualities, there are others whose professions make them connectors. Take a close look at the journalists, politicians, public relations specialists, bloggers, lawyers, lobbyists, charity fundraisers, recruiters, and human resources managers with whom you cross paths from time to time. All of them should go on your priority contact list!

In addition to offering valuable advice, "capacitors" can introduce you to many useful people. University professors, bankers, museum curators, art gallery owners, writers, and artists typically attract interesting people who either already occupy positions within centers of power or are trying to get as close to them as possible.

If you still find it difficult to gain access to certain organizations, remember the "bridges" you need to build to reach them. Once you establish a reliable connection with a member of a particular profession, say, a doctor, it will be much easier to expand your contacts within that group, and people will begin referring you from one person to another.

As you build your network of contacts, you will meet people you definitely want to befriend regardless of the roles they might play, simply because you like them.

Finally, do not forget your "frozen" contacts, friends with whom you have not been in touch for a while. They are usually people with whom you went to school or worked, but they may also come from other periods of your life. Reconnect with them, ask about their work and what has changed in their lives, and then stay in touch regularly. Join the alumni associations of the schools you attended and get involved. For one of us, for example, degrees from several prestigious universities in different countries, including Harvard University in the United States, opened many "doors" around the world and gave a significant boost to building a circle of friends.

Pipeline of Contact Opportunities

For an intelligence officer, making contacts is daily, even "routine," work. The Development Circle must always be kept full. To become a true networking professional, you must treat every interaction as part of a pipeline of opportunities to develop new relationships.

Expanding your network of contacts will inevitably require you to step outside your comfort zone. In intelligence, an inability to work outside your comfort zone means that you are professionally unfit. An intelligence officer must be able to find common ground with anyone. Like many other skills, the ability to work effectively beyond your usual social environment is acquired and perfected through practice.

When you begin expanding your opportunities for contact beyond your familiar surroundings, you will face two main challenges. The first is the growing insularity of our everyday relationships within groups where we feel comfortable, despite the enormous possibilities offered by communications technology. In fact, virtual social networking platforms have made this problem worse. They have made it easier for social and professional groups to build online enclaves that cocoon people in familiar relationships with others who share their views and outlook on life. Anyone who does not share the group's prevailing opinion quickly becomes an outsider with no place among "our own," "right" people.

If you want to expand your network of contacts beyond its current boundaries, be prepared to explore new places and social circles and to break through the barriers between professional groups. You will have to accept that breaking through such barriers can be painful and that gaining acceptance in unfamiliar circles may take considerable time.

The other challenge is the need to form relationships with people higher than you on the social ladder. High social status usually comes with age. Each generation carries its own history, its own "code words," its own jokes, and its own idols. It takes courage to speak as an equal with someone who is twenty or more years older and often much wealthier as well. It takes a certain talent to capture that person's interest. To secure a lasting place as a friend in that person's life, you will have to find common interests for which age and social position do not matter. The continual search for this "common ground" that reduces social distance is the essence of "networking upward."

One category of people finds it easier than others to build relationships with high-status individuals: journalists and people in the arts. Their professions give them direct access to celebrities. Some even become their partners or lovers. Consider Camille Claudel, the student and later lover of the great French sculptor Auguste Rodin, or Paula Broadwell, who wrote a biography of the American general David Petraeus. The technique of "networking upward" is based on the fact that as people grow older and attain higher status, they come to prefer giving to receiving. A sincere interest in their experience can help you find a way to reach even people who seem inaccessible. Such networking requires creativity and courage, but it also develops those qualities. So do not be afraid to try: even failure will enrich you with invaluable experience.

Your strategy for building a pipeline of contact opportunities should be two-pronged. First, ask your friends to introduce you to their acquaintances. Now you understand why the "connectors" we discussed earlier are so useful. And do not forget to thank them for their help!

Second, visit places where you can meet new people more often, and participate more actively in different events, clubs, and online groups. Make it a regular practice to seek out places and events where you can meet interesting people. As a networker, you should compile and periodically update a list of them. Keep statistics on your contacts to determine which places and events offer the best opportunities to meet people.

Those Terrible 30%

At first glance, everything seems simple enough. But things are not quite so easy or smooth. This is the so-called "30% rule." It turns out that unless you make an extra effort, you will most likely lose up to 30% of the people in your network every year. Some acquaintances will leave town, get married, or simply forget about you; others will end the relationship for one reason or another. And so on. This rate of attrition applies not only to ordinary people but also to intelligence officers, who, given the large influx of contacts into their networks, often pass up many opportunities that do not seem especially promising. The "30% rule" means that simply to maintain your existing number of connections, every year you need to add new contacts equivalent to as much as 30% of the total. To put it bluntly, this is no easy task!

"Wholesale" Networking

To offset the inevitable loss of that 30%, you need to find a way to acquire many good contacts quickly. Leading an interesting project, for example, is an excellent way to legitimately and deliberately build connections with many new people in a short time. We call this "wholesale" networking. Take part in as many projects or events as possible that bring large groups of people together.

Do you not see any such opportunities or projects around you yet? Be creative: come up with something yourself! By "project," we mean any idea that brings people together, from a political discussion group to a running club. Any such project will contribute not only to developing your network but also to your own professional and personal growth!

Such undertakings usually come with a pleasant bonus: meeting interesting and important people. Here is an example from our own experience. Our desire to expand our network into the highest decision-making circles led us to found a software development company in the United States. We later continued this line of work in Russia.

From Quantity to Quality

Do not get the impression that the main goal of networking is to increase the number of contacts. At some point, your network will become so large that adding more and more people will simply be pointless. You will not have time to manage your relationships with all your numerous contacts properly. You will need to focus first on improving the quality of your network. For an intelligence officer, the quality of a network is more important than its quantity. The "Pareto principle" applies to intelligence as well: 80% of the results of intelligence work will come from fewer than 20% of available contacts, the most valuable sources. The question of quality brings you back to the fundamental challenge of prioritizing your contacts. You need to decide which ones you need most and why. This will also require you to filter your contacts and remove from the network those that do not "work." This is exactly what sales managers do, for example.

The same can be said of the quality of your relationships. At the very beginning of life together, as you and your partner adjust to each other, you may experience some friction in the relationship. Much the same "adjustment" occurs with people entering your network of acquaintances. Aimless connections characterized by ambiguity and misunderstanding will drain your energy and ultimately fail you at the very moment when you need to rely on them. Regularly analyzing and cleaning up your relationships by discarding connections that do not work will spare you many problems and needless headaches.

If you want to become a successful networker, you need to think strategically and be purposeful and effective. You must determine which relationships bring you closer to your goals in life and business, and invest in developing those relationships first. To improve the quality of your network, you must clearly understand the ins and outs of your relationship with each contact. "Giving up" useless or unhealthy relationships may seem cynical, but in reality we do it constantly, consciously or unconsciously. By letting go of relationships that do not work, you avoid potential disappointment and open up opportunities for new, more useful and promising contacts.

Creating "Dossiers" on Your "Cultivation Targets"

To make your networking successful, you must learn as much as possible about your contacts: their lifestyles, personalities, and motivations. In the professional jargon we used, there was a term, "obyektivka," meaning a brief background profile on a particular person. This person might be anyone, from a foreign minister arriving to discuss an important treaty to someone under police surveillance. When our minister prepares for negotiations with a counterpart, intelligence typically provides two documents: one outlining the other side's expected negotiating position and another containing personal information about the opponent, including strengths and weaknesses. The details range from accounts by diplomats who negotiated with that person before to the coffee they prefer, how many children and pets they have, and what they call their mistress. Not surprisingly, the second document is often much more interesting and sought-after than the first.

The same need to understand the person in front of you applies to every potential contact. You cannot truly develop a relationship if you are not interested in who the person is and what they need. Sincere curiosity and a desire to understand how your acquaintance thinks and acts remain the most reliable guarantee that the relationship will develop. Such genuine interest on your part will register automatically with the person's emotional sensors, fostering rapport and reciprocity. The more facts you learn about someone, the better you will understand their character, needs, and motives. That is precisely why, especially in the early stages of a relationship, you need to assemble a "skeleton of facts" about your "target." Without this "skeleton," it is often difficult to interpret someone's behavior correctly. Imagine that at a party you organized, a new contact pays excessive attention to women. Your opinion of him will differ radically depending on whether or not he is married.

Collecting information about your contacts should become an ongoing routine. Try to build a mental "model" of each person and understand what drives them in life, what makes them happy or angry, whom they love and cannot stand, what is happening at work, and the many other things that shape their actions. Inviting the person to your home, visiting theirs, or meeting their family will be especially productive for gathering invaluable facts that will help you understand them better.

Your Information Sources

Nowadays, there are countless ways to gather information about almost anyone. Most of us leave a substantial information trail, consisting of posts on online social networks and entries in numerous public databases. Start with a simple Google search. If you find some basic information - what the person does and where they work and live - look up the relevant organizations and examine maps of the surrounding areas. This information will help you imagine what surrounds the person every day, what they have to deal with, and how they might feel. Simply put yourself in their shoes...

As always, however, the best and most up-to-date information comes from people, and the closer they are to the person who interests you, the better. The simplest and most reliable way to understand someone is to ask people who know them. There is nothing wrong with asking such questions, provided you can explain why you need the information. If you want to meet the person, there is your reason. Just go and do it! As intelligence officers, we had to take special care not to arouse suspicion, but for everyone else it is much simpler. In fact, your questions may even pique the curiosity of the person you are asking.

When the police or security services need more information about a suspect but must obtain it covertly, without attracting attention, they devise rather elaborate ways to talk to the suspect's colleagues and neighbors, posing as couriers, sociologists, former girlfriends, and so on. You would be surprised by how much a neighbor might tell a stranger about you, provided the stranger knows how to ask the right questions. During intelligence-training exercises, we conducted dozens of these "neighbor interviews." Each one required the role-play scenario to be worked out thoroughly so that we were prepared to respond to every possible answer.

In many cases, the meeting place itself provides some basic information that helps you make contact. Conferences and other subject-specific events are expressly designed to give people an opportunity to meet one another. Along with coffee breaks, contact-information booths, and designated meeting areas, such events provide detailed information about speakers and attendees, giving everyone an opportunity to visit the relevant website before meeting in person and form a preliminary impression of them. It makes sense to scan the attendee list, identify people who might interest you in advance, and read about the speakers. All this will make your networking more focused and your first conversations more productive. Becoming a speaker at an event will further increase your chances of being noticed and making new acquaintances.

Chapter 3. First Contact

Every relationship between people begins with a first contact, whether in person or online. As the saying goes, "You never get a second chance to make a first impression." First contact is even more important for an intelligence officer: failure to make the right first impression may do more than prevent you from earning someone's trust or, say, earning more money. It often dooms a carefully planned operation and nullifies the efforts of many people.

Thanks largely to Hollywood, most people imagine intelligence officers as cold, calculating, excessively reserved, and cautious. Think of Scarlett Johansson as Black Widow in the popular Marvel Comics franchise. In truth, however, the exact opposite is the case! The most successful intelligence officers are generally pleasant, friendly, and extremely open people.

Perhaps this is the rare instance in which James Bond's portrayal is not so far from the truth. His charm and powers of seduction are far more effective than any ingenious gadget from Q that he keeps concealed on his person. Interestingly, the overwhelming majority of neighbors of exposed intelligence officers whose names later became public considered them some of the best people they had ever met... And that is how it should be! To become a good intelligence officer - or a good networker - it is very important to know how to make a positive impression. Rufina Pukhova-Philby, the Russian wife of the famous Englishman Kim Philby, who collaborated with Soviet intelligence for many years, said that her husband was so charming that "you wanted to tell him everything." In Moscow, he taught young intelligence officers the art of being charming. He devised role-playing exercises. He himself would play either a representative of the Ministry of Foreign Affairs or a border guard officer.

That is why so much of an intelligence officer's training is specifically designed to maximize effectiveness at first contact. Our training usually included exercises such as approaching a random stranger and "fishing out" a specific piece of information.

For an intelligence officer, preparation for a "planned contact" may begin long before meeting the person face to face. That is why we divide all initial contacts into two categories: prepared and spontaneous. They differ not only in the time you must spend forming an opinion of the person you are meeting, but also in the level of risk, which is largely symbolic for ordinary people but very substantial for an intelligence officer.

First Meeting Scenarios

There are two possible scenarios for a prepared initial contact. The first and best way to establish contact is, of course, to have someone introduce you. Ideally, the intermediary introducing you to the person should do so in person. The intermediary's presence is essential: your friend vouches for you, immediately creating a degree of trust in the eyes of the person you are meeting. Having several such "connectors" among your friends will ultimately give you a steady flow of new contacts with whom, as the saying goes, the ice has already been broken. You can observe an interesting "one of us" effect when you use a club or professional association as a platform for making contacts. As an insider - a member of the club or community - you benefit from this sense of camaraderie, which allows you to skip the earliest stage of building a relationship, when no emotional connection has yet developed between the people involved. For example, when someone brings a newcomer to our cigar club, by the end of the evening you may hear the new acquaintances animatedly discussing business plans or trips together. They are demonstrating a level of trust that would otherwise be simply unthinkable after a first meeting.

In the second scenario, after carefully assessing the person, you use or create circumstances in which you and your "target" can strike up a conversation. In either scenario, it is best to learn as much as possible about the person's character and interests. This will allow you to prepare several topics that will interest your contact and help sustain the conversation. Your questions should give the other person an opportunity to demonstrate their knowledge and status and to feel that you appreciate both. Such questions should also provide a reason for further contact.

How to Overcome Fear of Talking to a Stranger

No matter how well you prepare, you will naturally worry that your first contact may fall through. There is no point in fighting these fears or trying to avoid them! Even after thousands of spontaneous contacts, we still feel nervous before the next meeting. Although this fear can disorient and demotivate you, it can also sharpen your focus and ultimately help you achieve more. A healthy, normal attitude toward fear and stress can become an invaluable asset in every area of life.

One of us once recalled the fear they felt before their first parachute jump. The ground was far, far below, and stepping into the void seemed physically unnatural. Then a thought occurred to them: almost everyone who had made the same leap into the abyss had ultimately landed safely. In this game of chance, the odds were firmly on the side of success. Yes, there are moments when you simply have to believe... At that moment, faith in success helped them tame their fear. The same faith later helped them make many "cold" contacts.

Over time, you will learn to manage your fear of approaching people. Spontaneous contacts are essentially the same kind of lottery. Some attempts are bound to fail. Sometimes you will receive a flat rejection that painfully bruises your ego and leaves a painful memory. But the overwhelming majority of interactions will work as they should. We guarantee it! So keep going and take the leap!

How to Prevent Rejection

During a conversation, you will have to balance two opposing objectives: getting the person interested in you and avoiding provoking rejection. No contact will develop if the person who interests you does not want to deal with you or, worse, is afraid of you. You must create the right conditions to engage the person in conversation.

Most of the advice you will find in networking books concerns making contacts during a coffee break at a conference or at a party, when everyone is receptive to meeting people. Intelligence officers, however, often have to work in settings where approaching new people is difficult or unwelcome. Successfully engaging people in conversation under such circumstances calls for an entirely different level of skill.

The main thing that prevents someone from fully engaging with you is uncertainty about who you are and what you want. On meeting you for the first time, people consciously or unconsciously assess the risks and opportunities that might arise from knowing you. What do you want from this new connection? What if you ask for something the person cannot or does not want to give? Could associating with you damage the person's reputation? Are you a boring or interesting conversationalist? Can you provide useful connections? A professional intelligence officer therefore prepares specifically to dispel the other person's concerns as quickly as possible. The key question an intelligence officer must answer convincingly is, "Why are you approaching me?" Until you give the other person a clear reason for your interest, they will feel uncomfortable with you. The professional's art lies in finding or devising such a reason.

Not long ago, I witnessed how ignoring the rule above can ruin a seemingly guaranteed contact. A friend suggested that the two of us meet with an "interesting person," the local head of a well-known international club. Knowing that my friend was an active "connector," I assumed that we would discuss involving me in the club's work. Events then followed the most "disastrous" possible course. First, the "connector" did not show up for the meeting and made little effort even to excuse his absence. Second, the "interesting person" was very late. He then began talking about himself, his projects, and his interests. The club came up only in passing and only at my prompting. Most importantly, I never understood why he had wanted to meet me. Throughout our conversation, he made no attempt to find out what might interest me and offered nothing. My solitary cup of coffee on the table did not encourage a long conversation either. We parted fully aware that there would be no follow-up.

You will also provoke rejection if you take too much initiative when it is unwelcome. Here is a little personal experience. Many, many years ago, as ambitious operatives, we often suffered from excessive ambition. We wanted quick results, and it showed clearly in our behavior. Looking back on those early years from the vantage point of experience, it is embarrassing to admit that we probably spoiled quite a few good contacts by behaving too "pushily"!

Here is one example from our many years of experience. On arriving in the United States, we became part of an exclusive community at one of America's leading universities. We had an extraordinary opportunity to make a great many useful contacts. Many of our classmates could potentially become important political figures, employees of government or military organizations, or even leaders of foreign countries. A true gold mine for an intelligence officer! Because the program lasted only one year, we felt that there was no time to lose. After identifying several "targets," we began deliberately developing relationships with them. Our very first contact - with a student who had a military background - seemed especially promising. Encouraged by his initial reaction, we used every opportunity to deepen the relationship: we stayed to chat after class, invited him to lunch, met him for coffee, and visited local attractions together. Encouraged by the apparent progress, we failed to consider his need to socialize with other people as well, especially at the beginning of his studies, when relationships among classmates were still forming. We later learned that he was also romantically interested in one of the female students. In our eagerness to become his "best friends," we did not immediately realize that our persistence was beginning to have the opposite effect. Very soon, he simply began to avoid us. The relationship dwindled to nothing and, despite all our efforts, we were unfortunately unable to restore it. Our acquaintance distanced himself from us and preferred closer relationships with other students. Fortunately, we had much greater success in our later contacts, when we tried to build more balanced relationships with people.

"Behavioral Hygiene"

When you meet someone for the first time, you need to give them enough time to get a good look at you, assess you, and grow accustomed to your presence. If you are in the same room, try to spend some time within the potential contact's field of vision so that they can observe you. If you plan to speak to them, begin with a predictable and positive topic. It is hardly surprising that many conversations between strangers begin with banal remarks about the weather - it is what it is!

Besides trying to allay the other person's fear when you enter their personal space during a spontaneous contact, you need to pay particular attention to the rules of so-called "behavioral hygiene." Your contact should not feel threatened or uneasy. For example, if you turn up at an art gallery intoxicated and talk loudly, you will make yourself a negative "attractor." You will become the "odd one out" in the eyes of those around you and provoke a defensive reaction.

Take steps to eliminate every possible "impression killer," from bad breath and dirty fingernails to talking too much, speaking excessively loudly, or making the uncontrolled leg movements that are common when people are nervous. Last but not least, without a smile and a relaxed posture, you are unlikely to make a positive impression!

Try a simple test: sit for ten minutes somewhere in a shopping mall or café and observe the people around you. Some will be sitting; others will walk past. Divide them into two groups: those you would like to meet and those you have absolutely no desire to approach. You will agree that the people in the first group have relaxed, confident, smiling faces. That is how you should look if you want to attract someone.

People feel most comfortable with those who resemble them and whom they understand. Ideally, you should dress and behave like a member of the other person's social group, whether it is a business group or something else. If you manage to learn something about the person's occupation or interests before making contact, you might bring along a book related to their profession. This too can be a useful conversational aid.

Had we written this book a generation ago, our standard recommendation would have been to dress "well" - conservatively and even expensively. Today, however, when even billionaires wear nothing but T-shirts, that old advice makes little sense. Instead, we recommend looking "recognizable," so that your "target" can easily associate you with a respectable social type. With a high-end laptop or tablet in your hands, for example, you could easily pass for an IT professional. Many social classes and professions have their own unofficial uniforms: venture capitalists wear Patagonia vests, bankers wear expensive dark-blue pinstriped suits, and professors wear tweed blazers with leather elbow patches. Let us hope, in any event, that you are not holding a bloody knife or a smoking gun; otherwise, your promising contact will flee in terror...

If you cannot look like someone from your contact's field, at least try not to alarm them with flamboyant clothing or possessions (unless you happen to be a movie star or some other important person). If you want to strike up a conversation with the person in the next seat on an airplane, wait until they have settled in, relaxed, and begun taking an interest in their surroundings. To play along a little, try mirroring their preflight routine: take out a few magazines, a pen, or your glasses; check the messages on your phone. Or do your neighbor a small favor without drawing particular attention to yourself: pass them a bag or a napkin. The moment when the flight attendants serve drinks and food throughout the cabin is especially convenient for starting a conversation.

The Art of Spontaneous Contacts

Sometimes you will have no choice but to start a conversation without knowing anything about the person in front of you. Spontaneous contacts like these require particular skill. The main challenge is ensuring that the interaction continues beyond the first meeting. You will have to keep a conversation going with a stranger while learning enough about the person's background, interests, or character to suggest another meeting.

Unfortunately, that is not the only challenge. When we become desperate to interest someone, many of us unconsciously begin to "advertise" ourselves. We talk about how smart and rich we are and how many important "big shots" we know. And we fail. As novice intelligence officers, we repeatedly fell into psychological traps like this. Yes, it can sometimes help to "sell" yourself a little in order to attract someone, but that is only a secondary task.

Instead, you should focus on getting the person to "buy you." The goal is for your contact to enjoy dealing with you. People are governed by emotions. Creating pleasant feelings for the other person during a conversation means showing that you like them. You need to set your ego aside and give the person in front of you your sincere, undivided attention, showing genuine interest. Make an effort and try almost to love this person. Everyone, even someone very shy, likes to talk about themselves. You have surely heard someone described as the most pleasant person and the best conversationalist simply because they did not interrupt the speaker. Think about that! How do you suppose psychotherapists create an atmosphere in which a patient feels closeness and trust?

At first contact, you need to focus entirely on the reaction of the person who interests you. The objective is to develop and deepen the interaction and, for heaven's sake, avoid driving away a potential contact. You must be able to read the response to your words and gestures and determine clearly whether it permits the conversation to continue. The response need not be verbal. Polite phrases may conceal a demand to "leave me alone," conveyed through tone or gestures. To gauge the person's true response, tune your perceptions to the emotional "temperature" of the interaction.

Awareness that our movements and unconscious actions make up a significant part of interpersonal communication has become so widespread that the term "body language" is now firmly established in the modern lexicon. As intelligence officers, we spent years practicing the subtle art of appearing approachable while simultaneously "reading" the other person's unconscious signals.

Many books offer advice on reading signs of "body language." Some authors even cite their experience working in the intelligence or security services. As professionals with many years of operational experience, however, we are frankly skeptical about the practical value of such advice for a novice networker. Most physical reactions are highly specific not only to a culture but also to a situation. For example, nearly universal gestures of goodwill and invitations to begin a conversation - such as a smile and direct eye contact - may be considered excessively intrusive or even embarrassing in some cultures. Interpreting other, less obvious signs becomes harder still when people's national backgrounds and patterns of social behavior reflect multiple cultures. In addition, we are taught from childhood to be polite and to suppress our true emotional responses, especially around strangers.

Rather than trying to recognize particular signs, a good networker must be able to pick up and interpret the entire stream of behavioral signals coming from a person during an interaction. Individual signals matter less than the overall picture. If you watch the emotional state of those around you carefully, you will probably detect shifts in mood - irritation, boredom, haste, or disapproval - in a person from almost any culture and be able to respond accordingly.

Synchronizing Perceptions

During an interaction, your task is to align your perceptions with the other person's feelings - to see yourself as they see you. This requires empathy. One example of such emotional attunement is an interviewer who, while listening closely, unconsciously mirrors the other person's posture and the tilt of their head. To read another person's emotions, you need to draw on your emotional intelligence.

You have probably already heard of emotional intelligence (EI). Unlike "traditional" intelligence, which measures how "smart" a person is, EI focuses on a person's ability to be "wise" in dealing with their own emotions and those of others. Daniel Goleman's book on the subject is excellent. He was among the first to draw attention to the fact that a person's ability to understand others on an emotional level has a greater effect on success at work than professional skills do.

Emotional intelligence is our ability to understand our emotions, where they come from, and what influences them. On the one hand, awareness of your emotional state enables you to control and manage your own behavior. On the other, it enables you to read other people's emotions correctly and behave in a way that suits the moment and the social setting. You have probably encountered speakers who plow on with boring presentations without noticing that half the audience is already nodding off. Such speakers do not know how to "read" the audience. They do not sense the audience on an emotional level or "empathize" with what it is experiencing.

In networking, you need to keep your eyes and ears open so that you can read another person's emotional state, detect changes in behavior, and adjust your strategy accordingly. For an intelligence officer, constantly monitoring their own behavior and the other person's reactions becomes second nature. They live as though a GoPro camera were mounted on every contact's head, continuously receiving an audio and video feed that allows them to spot their own mistakes.

As a practical exercise for developing your emotional intelligence, try spending a day with the "camera on," analyzing your own perceptions and the feelings of the people around you. You will have plenty to think about!

By giving the other person your full attention, you also address another problem people often mention during an initial spontaneous contact: how to avoid forgetting the person's name. When someone says they could not remember the names of people they met at a party or meeting, it usually means remembering those names was not very important to them. We have never heard of anyone forgetting a new boss's name.

To be honest, neither of us has any special talent for remembering names. There is, however, a simple three-step method that helps. First, after hearing the other person's name, try to use it several times in the next few sentences of the conversation. If you still forget it, ask again. There is nothing embarrassing about that when you have only just met. Second, try giving the person a nickname in your own mind based on how they look, what they do, or how you met; associations like these stimulate the memory. Finally, write the name in a notebook or on your phone, and ask for a business card at the end of the conversation. This will not only engage your visual memory but also stroke the other person's ego by showing that they made an impression on you.

Initial Sorting - The "OIS" Method

In a spontaneous encounter, you do not have the luxury of advance information about the person. Such information helps you prepare good conversation topics that may interest them and make sure that interacting with them will not create problems later. This is precisely why spontaneous encounters require an intelligence officer to "analyze" and "sort" the people they meet using the "OIS" method.

When meeting someone for the first time, a professional intelligence officer must answer three critically important questions, whether there are months or only seconds before the handshake: Is this person dangerous? Are they interesting? Are they difficult?

"OIS" preserves the Russian initials of those questions: Opasen? ("Dangerous?"), Interesen? ("Interesting?"), and Slozhen? ("Difficult?"). The English descriptors do not form the acronym; OIS is retained as the source-language name of the method.

This framework is very useful when you find yourself in the middle of a welcome reception at a conference or, for example, at a distant relative's birthday party. In either case, you are surrounded by strangers.

Let us begin with O, Opasen ("Dangerous"). If an intelligence officer is connected with, or even briefly seen in the company of, people wanted by the police or involved in extremist organizations, the result may be surveillance or an investigation, potentially with catastrophic consequences. The same applies to encounters with counterintelligence personnel, criminal police officers, diplomats, or people who work in classified government laboratories. Accidental, unprepared contact with such people can easily lead to investigation and arrest.

For those far removed from the world of spies, the list of dangerous contacts is not especially short either. Do you want to be associated with people who express hatred and threaten others? Or with people who sell drugs? If you see or sense potential trouble, the answer is obvious: simply leave. You may be surprised by how many emotionally unstable people are around you. Their mood can change instantly, especially if you begin arguing with them. If you keep the conversation going in the hope of calming them down, they may latch on to you like ticks, after which shaking them off will be almost impossible. If you think you are unlikely to meet such people in your own circles, do not be so sure. Think again!

While we were still in training, one of our mentors, who had worked undercover in Asia for more than ten years, told us a story. It underscored the need to be extremely careful when assessing the people with whom you come into contact. At a hotel bar in a Southeast Asian capital, he met a man who looked every inch a prosperous European businessman. After a few drinks, they discovered common ground: both were new to the country and both planned to start their own businesses. The opportunity to invest in a venture with a "respectable gentleman" who, by his own account, already "had influential friends in the country" seemed too attractive to pass up. From the first moment they met, however, something put our intelligence officer on guard. By the end of the evening, he finally understood what it was: the gentleman's fingernails were unkempt, with dirt clearly visible underneath them. A follow-up check revealed that he was a well-known local con artist. By day, he worked as a gardener to make ends meet; in the evening, he switched to his "main business"...

For those who pass the O-test, Opasen ("Dangerous"), there is a second stage of assessment: I, Interesen ("Interesting"). Not all contacts are created equal. Your time and resources are limited, so you must be pragmatic. If, after talking for a while, you see no potential value in the contact, pause and think. You can either put work with this contact "on ice" for now, recording the details so that you can decide later, or decide against pursuing the contact and bring the conversation to a skillful close.

Ending a conversation gracefully is an art in itself. If you handle it badly, the other person will feel that you treated them with disdain and will never forgive you for it. Your exit from a conversation must be carefully prepared and suited to the setting. We are sure you have experienced people who "latch on" to you at the beginning of a party and will not let go for hours, almost physically preventing you from speaking with anyone else. You probably hate such situations as much as we do. Besides, people like that kept us from doing our work! Over time, we developed a whole repertoire of excuses, ranging from a simple "Excuse me, I need to use the restroom" to phone calls to one another that we presented as urgent requests from foreign business partners. We prompted those calls with secret signals known only to the two of us. We suggest arranging something similar with a close friend or partner.

Finally comes S, Slozhen ("Difficult"). Unfortunately, it can be very difficult to build relationships even with highly interesting people. This may be due to significant differences in age or social standing, but the most common reason is a lack of the right "chemistry." With experience, you may begin to recognize at a very early stage of an interaction that the chemistry simply is not there. If you continue the contact in the same vein, the relationship will drag on without meaningful progress in its development or in mutual trust. It will be awkward, dull, formal, artificial, and difficult to maintain. Nothing useful will come of it. We are sure you have experienced relationships like these. If there is no compelling reason to preserve one, apart from family or financial obligations, let it go. You will feel relieved and, incidentally, save yourself time. Again, if you doubt that you can benefit from the relationship, simply take the person's contact details and move on.

How to Start a Conversation with Different Types of People

Spontaneous encounters raise the question of how to start a conversation with different types of people. Describing the basic personality types and the nuances of communicating with them would require a separate book. It is nevertheless worth considering how to approach the most common types, which you will be able to recognize before making contact or during the opening stages of a conversation. Without delving into scientific terminology, we will draw on our own operational experience.

The easiest people to approach are "great communicators." They are ready to talk to you without any prompting on your part. They usually have a smile on their face, while a restless curiosity about everything around them shines in their eyes. They cannot sit still without talking to someone. All you need to do is avoid frightening them off. A smile in their direction, a nod, or a casual comment is enough to "switch them on." They will gladly talk about themselves; you need only guide the conversation by asking the right questions. The problem is how to "switch them off" afterward if you have no interest in continuing the contact. For understandable reasons, unfortunately, there are not many people in positions of power among the "great communicators," and even fewer people entrusted with secrets.

Another type consists of people who want to be the "center of attention." You will recognize them by their distinctive clothing, conspicuous "theatricality," or original choice of ties, gadgets, or jewelry. They need outside attention and admiration to affirm their self-worth. They do everything they can to be noticed, although that does not necessarily mean they want to strike up a conversation. The key to starting a conversation with such people is a compliment that shows them their efforts to be original and unique have been noticed and properly appreciated. Once the conversation begins, they will gladly talk about themselves. Better still if they catch your interested, admiring glance before the conversation even starts!

The first two types are naturally expressive and ready to keep a conversation going themselves. The next two, by contrast, generally present a real challenge at first contact. One of these difficult types is the "controller," whom you can identify by a serious, focused gaze and by the position they choose, which allows them to monitor everything happening around them. This person is usually a perfectionist who values living by rules and often tries to impose those rules on others. At first contact with a "controller," your strategy is to demonstrate that you also value order, understand the rules the person follows, and perhaps even show that you are willing to accept them. In the first conversation, then, focus your questions on discovering how the other person sees the world and what is "good" or "bad" within their frame of reference, while allowing them to "impose" their perspective on you. A critical observation about an obvious problem, such as a delayed flight, may be an effective way to start a conversation with a perfectionist-controller...

Finally, the most difficult type to approach is the "closed-off" person. Such a person is usually in a posture of "all-around defense" against the rest of the world. They trust no one and look for a catch in everything. In public, they are shy, silent, and absorbed in their own thoughts. You are unlikely to start a conversation with a "closed-off" person without a clear and important reason that justifies the risky step outside their "fortress." Even asking for help does not guarantee a response. Starting a conversation with such a person therefore often resembles a psychotherapist speaking with a patient. Your main task is to put them at ease. Moreover, because of their high anxiety, even physically approaching close enough to start a conversation requires time and tact. The person must have enough time to adapt their world to your presence. One of us once had to spend almost a week at the next table in a city library before speaking to one such "closed-off" person for the first time!

How to Present Yourself Properly

The person you are meeting will almost certainly first think, "Who is this person?" They will immediately begin mentally screening you, using a process like the OIS test described earlier. Your task is to pass that screening successfully. Their first impression of you will be visual, so we strongly recommend eliminating every possible "red flag" from your appearance and, where possible, presenting yourself in a similar way to the person you are speaking with.

As soon as you start the conversation, you will need to say something about yourself, explaining what you are doing there and why you want to talk. You must give the person a good reason to have a conversation. In intelligence jargon, such an explanation is called "situational cover." We suggest rehearsing it in advance so that it sounds smooth and answers the immediate questions that arise when a stranger approaches someone.

A line such as "...I have just finished a consulting project and would like to see the city center before I fly to London tomorrow" may "work" perfectly well. It creates several favorable impressions in the person's mind. You are a consultant. You probably have a good education and a decent income, so you are not about to ask for a loan. You seem well traveled and might therefore be an interesting conversationalist. You are curious and have a little free time, which explains why you started the conversation.

Incidentally, when preparing your "cover story" for starting a conversation with a stranger, we advise you not to lie about yourself. If you want to make a better impression and add details that are not true, remember that strangers sometimes become close friends and may later hear the whole truth about you from someone else!

How to Manage a Spontaneous Conversation

If you have enough information about the person you are meeting, topics and questions prepared in advance will allow you to guide the discussion. To start and sustain a conversation with someone you do not know, however, you will need other approaches.

No secret formula has yet been invented for starting and sustaining a conversation with someone you do not know. You must draw all your ideas from three sources: the person you want to contact, the circumstances of the meeting, and, finally, yourself. The art of talking with a stranger lies in using your imagination and making the most of this rather limited set of options.

What ideas can come from the "target"? First, you can offer help if the person has a problem. They may drop something, stumble, or their phone battery may die. Perhaps it only seems that the person has a problem, but even that will work as an opening. In either case, you can introduce yourself and offer help. Second, a person may do something or look in a way that invites a comment or compliment, which in turn may lead to a conversation. Almost anything can provide an occasion for a compliment, from a stylish tie to a beautiful smile. Let us hope the stranger does not take it as excessive forwardness, especially today, when everyone fears accusations of "harassment."

Another set of openings arises from the situation itself. If you are stuck in a cafe by the pouring rain outside or witness an accident, it is perfectly natural to voice an opinion or respond to someone else's comment. We still remember one of the first exercises in our intelligence training. We had to position ourselves near a painting in an art museum and try to strike up conversations with visitors by commenting on the work. We counted how many conversations lasting more than five minutes we could conduct in an hour. The assumption was that five minutes would be enough to find sufficient common ground with a stranger for the relationship to continue.

Now consider a couple of openings in which you yourself provide the pretext. You can ask a stranger a question or ask for help. Both approaches are fairly straightforward. Your "target's" reaction, however, depends on exactly what you ask and how. Your imagination is the only limit. Con artists and pickpockets have developed an enormous repertoire of tricks for attracting attention and starting conversations. You have probably experienced something like this: a well-dressed man calls out to you, points to a wallet on the sidewalk, and asks whether you dropped it. The act these con artists put on is so convincing that many of us end up spending several minutes talking to them. It can take us a while to realize that we are being conned...

What if the person does not respond to your question, or merely smiles and walks on? That is exactly what will happen if you suddenly spring on a stranger in the middle of the street. In fact, an intelligence officer preparing to make first contact aims to create a situation in which a question or request for help cannot be ignored. As a professional networker, you must choose your conversation-opening questions carefully. The harder they are to ignore, the better.

The same is true of keeping a dialogue going and holding your contact's attention. Listen to the "small talk" at a party and you will notice that every conversation revolves around a couple of hot news stories and a handful of standard topics of general interest. If you keep up with sports, cultural, and political news and know the most widely discussed issues, that should be enough to sustain a conversation with a new contact and steer it in the direction you want. We recommend keeping a mental "list" of popular topics to which you can always redirect the conversation.

How to Ensure the Next Stage of the Relationship

Once you have successfully "sustained" a conversation with a promising contact, your primary goal is to secure another meeting with that person. We hope you will use all your active-listening skills to make the person interested in talking with you. We also expect you to focus on your contact during the conversation rather than on yourself. We assume you have prepared some topics and questions for this first conversation that might interest the other person. If it turns out, for example, that the person supports a particular sports team, try to elicit a comment on the team's performance, at least if the team is doing well. We advise building up an ample store of topics and questions in advance so you can keep the conversation interesting. Before the conversation ends, your task is to work out what you can offer as a way of continuing the relationship that the person will not refuse. One way to build a "bridge" to the next meeting is to ask for some friendly advice or offer something the other person needs. At least at the first meeting, however, this "something" should not entail commitments that would require much of your new acquaintance's time or resources. Professionals call it a "lead" or a "hook," something that gives you a reason to contact the person again. Depending on your interests and capabilities, you should have a collection of such "leads."

One notorious trick used by intelligence services and criminals around the world is the so-called "dented bumper." Someone else's car quite literally collides with yours. The "culprit" apologizes profusely and offers not only to cover all the damage but also to invite you to dinner and give you a gift to erase the unpleasant memory from your mind. The obvious purpose is to create a plausible pretext for spending as much time with you as possible while building a relationship!

We are not, of course, suggesting that you damage someone else's car to start a relationship, but you should have several strategies in reserve. Depending on your "target," you might offer an introduction to an interesting person you know or share information on a subject important to your contact. Even if you do not have that information now, you will certainly find something worthwhile if you search carefully online or ask your friends. People rarely say "no" to such no-strings-attached offers. They may regard one as merely a courteous gesture, but for you it is a solid reason to continue the dialogue.

Follow Up or Lose It!

When the conversation is over, your primary task is to secure the next step. We strongly recommend getting back in touch with your new contact within 24 hours, or at least within 48 hours, of the meeting. We followed a simple rule: no matter what happened, we sent a "reminder" the morning after meeting someone. You need to remind the person of yourself before the memory of your conversation fades, especially if they had other meetings that day. In your call or message, be sure to mention whatever piqued the contact's interest and gives your next interaction a logical and sound basis, such as your promise to help with something.

As a professional networker, you must create a database of your contacts. This is not merely the contact list on your phone, with names, workplaces, and birth dates. You need to be able to record details of your conversations and track the progress of each relationship, as well as enter information about your contacts' backgrounds, interests, and distinctive traits. These details will later help you analyze your relationships and trace the source of any problems that arise. At the end of the book, we will provide a template you can use to create this kind of record. Such a database is invaluable to an intelligence officer. Incidentally, we suggest storing the contact information on a USB drive rather than on your computer, or even printing it and keeping it in a secure place. You never know what might happen to phones or computers these days, or who might obtain these invaluable notes if your account is hacked!

Corporations have long used specialized customer relationship management (CRM) software of varying complexity. If you like technology and gadgets, you can subscribe to some of these products as a service for managing your personal network of contacts for a relatively small fee.

And one more thing! Memories fade quickly; that is simply how human memory works. Within only a few hours, you are likely to forget many details of a conversation. So please make a habit of taking notes while the details are still fresh, preferably on the same day. You must make yourself do this even when you are very tired. Once you form the habit, the routine will no longer be a burden; it will become second nature.

If you think we are asking too much of you, compare this with the system devised by one of the world's greatest networkers, who was regarded as one of the leaders of the "secret world government": David Rockefeller, who died at the age of 101. As Joann Lublin writes in The Wall Street Journal, "Mr. Rockefeller recorded the contact information and details of every meeting he had with more than 100,000 people from around the world on 3-by-5-inch index cards. Altogether, he accumulated roughly 200,000 cards, which were loaded into a Rolodex machine custom-built for him. For more than half a century, this five-foot-tall electronic machine stood in his private office at Rockefeller Center in New York."

As the head of Chase Manhattan Bank and a prominent philanthropist, Rockefeller "kept track of a seemingly endless number of ties to the rich and famous, spanning borders, disciplines, and interests, from fellow business titan Bill Gates to the Shah of Iran, President John F. Kennedy, Pope John Paul II, and Neil Armstrong, the first person to walk on the Moon." "Thanks to this unusual record-keeping system, he could resume a conversation with someone he had not seen for years as though their last meeting had been the previous week." "Over lunch at his estate in 2015, Mr. Rockefeller gave Mr. Kissinger copies of the 35 Rolodex cards devoted to him. They contained notes on hundreds of their conversations dating back to their first meeting in 1955," Joann Lublin writes.

"Mr. Rockefeller came to understand the importance of contacts while serving in Army intelligence overseas during World War II," Lublin continues. "My effectiveness depended entirely on my ability to build a network of people with reliable information. I can quickly get to the essence of my past relationship with anyone before seeing them again," Mr. Rockefeller wrote in his 2002 memoirs.

What more can we add to convince you? Fortunately, there is no need today to invent special Rolodex systems for databases like this. Open a page and dictate; the computer will do the rest. But David Rockefeller's remarkable experience once again underscores the central idea of our book: if you work on your "ecosystem of connections" deliberately and systematically, networking will become a powerful tool capable of changing your life.

Chapter 4. Developing Relationships

Become a "Gardener" of Human Relationships

So, the first meeting went well. You now know each other. But building a meaningful relationship is a long journey, and many "minefields" lie in wait. As in a desert, you will see mirages from time to time. It may seem that the relationship is about to enter a new stage, only for you to realize that it is still far from its ultimate destination: mutual trust.

In movies, spies use sex, violence, and blackmail to achieve their goals. In real life, an intelligence officer's most powerful weapon is closeness and trust. On screen, spies crack safes to obtain secrets. In reality, other people do it for them: people the officers have befriended, like, and empathize with, and who consider being a friend and helping far more important than guarding someone else's secrets. Even people who sell secrets for money or pass on confidential information for some other reason rely on the intelligence officer's trust and emotional support, because their well-being and perhaps even their life now depend on that officer. They must feel certain that the person who knows more about them than anyone else will protect their secrets, respect them, and care for them. The great successes of every intelligence service have rested on bonds of affection, empathy, support, and friendship between agents and their handlers. This was true both of the famous aristocrats of the "Cambridge Five," who worked for the Soviet Union during World War II, and of Oleg Gordievsky, the head of the KGB First Chief Directorate's London station who spied for MI6. The intelligence officer's art lies in building relationships of trust, confidence, and dependence.

Relationships naturally span a whole spectrum of levels and shades, from casual acquaintances to close friends. When discussing what happens in the Productivity Circle, we noted that a relationship need not be very close to produce results that matter to you. Mutual trust is a delicate thing and difficult to achieve with every contact. For a relationship to be considered stable and productive, however, it must reach a certain level. An intelligence officer, businessperson, or any other networker then becomes a kind of "gardener" who "grows" relationships, protecting them from "winds" and "weeds" in the hope that one day they will bear the desired fruit.

Two Stages in Relationships

The process of building a relationship has two stages. The first lasts from the initial contact until the relationship becomes stable. This means that there is a clear reason for the people to continue interacting personally that is not tied to a particular event or transaction. The participants are not necessarily emotionally close, however. At this stage, you cannot expect the person to do something for you that would require great effort, much less risk, on their part.

The second stage is the deepening of the relationship. The people now truly open up to each other and become "accomplices," in the sense that they trust each other enough to share personal secrets and take business or personal risks together. Clearly, many relationships will never reach this second stage, but the closer they come to mutual trust, the more opportunities they create.

Some people seem simply made to be together, made to be friends. In most friendships, however, as in marriage, success depends on both the effort and the talent of the parties. The problem is that a talent for building relationships, like any other talent, is rarely distributed evenly. This is true even among intelligence officers. Some can "skim the cream" from a large number of contacts while sacrificing depth; others can extract the greatest possible depth and results from fewer connections. There are many cases in which one officer makes contact with a potential recruitment candidate and then hands the person over to a colleague for assessment and cultivation. They are like hunters and gardeners: each has different methods and a different path to success.

Do you know people who seem to know everyone and everything but appear incapable of building deep relationships or speaking with anyone "heart to heart"? Or perhaps you have only a few close friends, but you have known them since childhood and they will remain with you throughout your life. You probably already sense which part of the relationship-building process comes more easily to you and which skills you need to work on.

Do not equate sophisticated "socialites" with people who know how to develop useful connections effectively. Early in our operational career, for example, we envied people who excelled at party "small talk." We thought this was a skill we absolutely had to master to build the right relationships. The ability to carry on conversations "about nothing" is useful in itself, but our later experience showed that its importance should not be overstated. Champions of "small talk" are not necessarily effective at influencing others. If you want to hold another person's attention, it is far more useful to learn to ask good questions: people would rather express their own opinions than listen to yours. Not being the "most talkative" person at a party is no obstacle at all.

The process of deepening a relationship has its own dynamics. You may see a sharp rise in the intensity of your interactions, with numerous calls and meetings every day, but there may also be lulls when nothing happens. Problems and misunderstandings may arise whose causes no one can explain. Some meetings go well and others do not. Being in a relationship is like drifting down a river: you see the next bend only when you reach it. A holistic assessment of the relationship's dynamics is more useful than dwelling on the success or failure of any one conversation. Every meeting has its own context, purpose, and mood. In some cases, your partner may fake their behavior and mislead you in order to create the desired impression. But no one can fake an entire relationship.

Three "Legs" of Relationships

Relationships between people are like a chair: they need at least three "legs" for reliable support. The more "legs" your relationship has, the better, but three is the minimum that ensures its survival. The three "legs" are Common Interests, Emotional Empathy, and Common Social Circle. Some relationships, of course, rest on only two or even one of these three "legs," but they run a much greater risk of breaking. How many stories can you recall among your friends of two different worlds coexisting happily in the manner of "Beauty and the Beast"? Probably very few. How much time do you spend with people with whom you share no activities, interests, or compelling reasons to meet? They may be pleasant to chat with, but what happens next?

A common interest in a relationship implies the possibility of mutually beneficial exchange, whether of material benefits, business ventures, companionship, emotional support, or something else. If the common interest is unclear, the relationship's other two "legs" are often not enough to sustain it over the long term. The people meet less and less often, and their connection gradually fades.

Emotional Empathy is a mutual affection that helps "synchronize" personalities. It involves compatible temperaments and similar views and experiences. Such similarity does not mean being alike, however. For people seeking intellectual or interpersonal stimulation, opposites really can attract and enrich one another.

Common Social Circle can be defined in either a narrow or a broad sense. Both refer to inclusion in a shared environment, whether professional or purely social. In the narrow sense, it includes the active personal contacts in your Productivity Circle with whom you do business or spend your leisure time. In the broader sense, a Common Social Circle is a group of people with the same position in society, whether by income, education, or social status. Practical experience shows that people from socially dissimilar and distant groups maintain stable long-term relationships only through very specific activities or projects, such as hunting trips or visits to the bathhouse. As the old saying goes, "Birds of a feather flock together."

Stabilization and Consolidation of Relationships

At the very beginning, as you establish a relationship, your goal is to bring the person into your Productivity Circle and strengthen the connection so that it does not break at the first obstacle. The key word here is "stability." Early on, the context of a relationship is usually very specific. Imagine that someone agrees to meet you a second time because you promised an introduction to a venture investor. In that case, your contact sees you merely as a route to a particular goal. Even if the startup receives funding, you will simply be a participant in the contact's relationship with the investors. If the person does not receive the money, you will remain, in their eyes, "the person they wasted their time with." Your goal, therefore, is to move this budding relationship out of the specific context of an investment opportunity and into a so-called "neutral" state, in which the relationship is no longer tied to a particular deal, event, or promise. What now unites you depends only on what you both enjoy or do.

In this respect, consider the story of Soviet intelligence officer Richard Sorge, whom the English writer Tom Clancy called "the best spy of all time." Sorge's extraordinarily friendly manner, charisma, and gift for building connections helped him find common ground with even the most vigilant and difficult people. In 1933, after Adolf Hitler came to power in Germany, Sorge went to Japan using the "cover story" that he was a German journalist sympathetic to the Nazi regime. There he met Eugen Ott, the military attache at the German embassy, who later became Nazi Germany's ambassador to Imperial Japan. Over time, Sorge transformed the initially purely professional contact between an ambitious official and an interested, obliging reporter into a deep personal relationship spanning every aspect of life, including an affair with Ott's wife. Ott ultimately came to trust Sorge completely and became his principal source of critical information about the strategies and military plans of Japan and Germany. Sorge's accomplishments earned him a special place among the great intelligence officers.

"Exchange Currencies" of Relationships

It is time to address another myth about networking: the myth of "true friendship." We have heard it argued that relationships formed for a particular purpose, whether to get ahead in business or satisfy one's own ego, are somehow defective. Supposedly, relationships are either "real" or involve one party manipulating the other. The idea is that a relationship that is not wholly altruistic is actually "fake" and therefore somehow morally suspect.

Let us debunk this myth. Virtually all the connections in your Support Circle are free of material self-interest, unless a family business is involved. That is only natural. Look closely, however, at the relationships in your Productivity Circle, from very close friends to new acquaintances. Regardless of a relationship's depth, both you and the other person still have a particular reason to interact, and that reason is connected with some expected benefit. It may not be the only reason, or even the main reason, for maintaining the relationship, but it is present all the same. When that reason disappears, you will either realize that the person has entered your Support Circle, or your relationship will gradually weaken. Friendships are not divided into "true" and "false." They simply belong to different Functional Circles and are based on different goals.

Strong relationships are built on an exchange of something valuable. People are afraid to deepen a relationship if they do not understand what you might demand from them in the future. At some point, you need to state your "vision" for the relationship - that is, what you expect from your partner and what you can offer in return. This does not apply to the very first meetings, but do not put the matter off for too long: your acquaintance will want clarity in any event. If they do not share your vision, it is better to find out sooner rather than later.

Your "declaration of vision" does not necessarily have to be put into words. You can convey the message far more effectively through concrete actions. You should constantly look for something your contacts would value and actively offer it to them. When people see that you are bringing something important to them into the relationship, they will reciprocate.

Reciprocity is the sine qua non, the necessary condition for any strong relationship. If one party feels taken for granted or regarded as temporary, the relationship is bound to fail. The "exchange currency" can be anything - from enjoyable time together, friendly conversation, access to exclusive clubs, and mentoring, to help with career advancement or access to investment opportunities. You can be confident that a relationship is sound if you know exactly what you and your partner are exchanging and that the exchange benefits you both.

Three Parameters for Measuring Relationship Dynamics

How can you measure progress in a relationship and monitor its dynamics? Use your network map to track how a person moves from the Development Circle to the Productivity Circle, how the intensity of the relationship grows, and how they build connections with your friends. This will allow you to assess the stability of your connections and the progress you are actually making. Later in the book, we will offer several tools to help you do so. For now, however, we need a little theory. Relationships have several parameters that describe what is happening between you and your acquaintance. Let us consider them in turn.

Leadership and dependence characterize the parties' initiative and roles in an interaction. If you initiate the contact, it is only natural that you will initially find yourself managing the process. Any reversal of roles - for example, when your contact begins to take the initiative by suggesting that you go somewhere together or by calling you first - shows an interest in you and marks progress. Relationships in which initiative and leadership remain asymmetric for a long time usually fail, except when the roles are purely functional or one person naturally dominates by virtue of age and experience, as in a professor-student or employer-employee relationship.

The flow of communication in a relationship is another element that requires close attention. Infrequent communication means that the relationship has an unstable foundation: the participants have little to talk about. More frequent communication covering a wide range of topics - especially when it shows increasingly clear signs of sincerity, spontaneity, and emotion - is compelling evidence of growing closeness. Interaction between spouses within a family provides a good example of communication at its most intensive.

The opposite trend, in which communication becomes less frequent, should concern you: something is going wrong. The exceptions may be close friends or relatives who live far apart. During our intelligence career, we were unable to maintain regular contact with our relatives for almost three decades, but they were ready to welcome us with open arms at any time.

Emotional involvement and the level of mutual trust are also very important parameters of human relationships. As mutual understanding develops, people empathize with and support one another more, show greater emotional openness, and more often exchange views not only on everyday interests but also on moral, religious, and philosophical questions.

On the scale of personal emotional involvement - the best indicator of the depth and quality of human relationships - relationships range from casual acquaintances and blind dates to lifelong friendship or love. As we develop a relationship, we increase our participation and involvement in another person's life. We not only reduce the emotional distance between us but also expand the range of areas in which we interact.

As a rule, the roles of the participants gradually develop and expand as a relationship deepens. Consider a simple example. When you first meet someone, you know exactly what issue brought you together and what role each of you plays. You may be a buyer or seller, an intern or trainer. In a marriage, by contrast, it is difficult to find an area of life that does not require your joint participation. You are simultaneously a relative, a sexual partner, and a business partner...

"Peaches," "Apples," and "Pomegranates"

The dynamics of relationship development can also vary with the nationality of the person involved. In an era of globalization, national traits are gradually fading within business circles. Nevertheless, although typical behavior in relationships more often reflects social status than nationality, ethnic and national origin still matters. In the course of our work, we encountered three models of relationship development associated with national characteristics. We will call them, somewhat arbitrarily, "peaches," "apples," and "pomegranates."

In the "peach" model, a person easily establishes first contact and readily develops the relationship to the level of a casual acquaintance with no obligations. Considerable time and effort are then required to get the person to open up and to earn their trust. To the "touch," such a person resembles a peach: they are very soft, and their skin and flesh are easy to pierce, but then you encounter something very hard and impenetrable. Most Americans fit this model. They are very friendly and sociable, yet they are also extreme individualists who carefully guard their personal space.

Compared with American "peaches," Russians are more like "pomegranates." They form relationships with new people far less enthusiastically and are reserved and cautious in the early stages. Once the hard Russian "rind" has been pierced, however, almost no corner of their soul will remain closed to you!

The French, like many other Europeans, usually fall somewhere between "peaches" and "pomegranates" - something like "apples." More reserved than Americans at the initial stage, they prefer not to rush until mutual understanding and trust have matured. Nevertheless, some areas - like the seeds of an apple - will remain hidden and difficult to reach.

Of course, the three models in their pure form are a product of our personal experience. A person's character, motives, and background often play the dominant role in how a relationship develops.

How to Maintain Numerous Connections

The need to devote substantial time and emotional energy to relationships naturally limits the number of very close connections that can be maintained effectively. This becomes obvious as soon as you draw your Functional Circles. An ordinary person may have approximately 100 connections in the Development Circle, where more distant contacts are located, and up to 75 friends and colleagues in the Productivity Circle. Excluding relatives, people rarely have more than three to five very close, intimate friends in the Support Circle. For professionals who work extensively with people - intelligence officers, real estate agents, lawyers, or politicians, for example - the number of connections in the first two circles may be twice as high. Virtual contacts on Facebook or LinkedIn will add hundreds of contacts to your network, but the pattern of your online interaction will remain the same: you will still devote significant attention to only a small number of connections.

When you have a hundred people in your network, maintaining contact with them is not especially difficult. But what happens when a politician has to think about thousands of contacts, almost every one of whom regards the politician as an important connection and therefore expects attention? This brings one of networking's perhaps overused concepts to the fore once again: prioritization. Not all relationships are equal. Their value rises and falls as your life changes, and your objectives change along with it. Strategic networking returns us to the need to see your network from a "bird's-eye view." This means classifying your relationships and compiling several contact lists with different priority levels.

The first list consists of the key relationships on which your career, income, family stability, and most important future plans depend. You owe the people in this group regular personal attention. On average, you should reserve at least one extended meeting per quarter for them - preferably over lunch or dinner - as well as meaningful gifts for their birthdays and when you visit them at home. In addition, maintaining these relationships properly requires at least one planned phone call or electronic message per month, not counting "likes" on their social media posts.

The second list is the complete opposite of the first. It includes people with whom relationships can, to use a less than politically correct term, be "frozen." Little happens in these relationships from day to day, but they are easy to revive when necessary. Here you can use the full power of online social networks. Even so, a couple of phone calls a year to exchange news and offer birthday greetings are still essential to maintaining these relationships.

The third list contains everyone who falls outside the first two: new acquaintances from your studies or good friends to whom you feel indebted. Because these relationships are often still developing, your approach will depend on the stage and purpose of each one. Our experience suggests that stabilizing and strengthening an established relationship requires two or three meetings at which you raise issues of importance to both parties, followed by regular calls and emails addressing the topic you discussed as well as personal matters and interests.

When people meet and a friendship begins to develop, they communicate regularly and often. They grow accustomed to and actively learn about one another. Each forms a mental image of the other and adapts their behavior to the new friend. As mutual understanding grows, the interval between meetings can increase. Properly maintaining a relationship in the development stage may require anywhere from two meetings a month to one every two months, depending on the dynamics of emotional involvement. Relationships that are already stable and sufficiently close can withstand longer breaks without losing their significance.

Remote Networking

Years of travel and extensive experience moving our "operational base" from one country to another gave us a global network spanning every continent. Maintaining relationships with people on the other side of the world has always been difficult, but modern means of communication have made the task considerably easier in recent years.

We would like to emphasize that networking online and networking in the real world are very similar, whatever anyone may tell you. Online space is simply an extension of your real network, and the approaches we have discussed apply equally well online. Today's popular platforms and tools may change, but the dynamics of relationships among people and communities remain broadly the same. From a purely technical standpoint, building a network on Myspace is not fundamentally different from using platforms such as Instagram or WhatsApp. Let us therefore focus on methods and goals rather than on tools that constantly change.

Over the years, we have noticed that online relationships generally fall into the same three stable groups, depending on their purpose, frequency of interaction, and emotional closeness. These online groups mirror our Functional Circles. The key is to recognize the type of interaction you have with someone online and align your actions with your overall networking strategy.

The most intensive and intimate online interaction is usually "reserved" for members of your Support Circle and takes place almost every day. This circle often contains close-knit groups of friends engaged in an endless conversation, through a group chat, for example. The group is stable because all its members know about one another's daily lives. In this setting, our personal social "filter" is inactive, and we feel that we are part of a shared "zone of trust." This is precisely how deep interpersonal relationships are cultivated online.

The next type of online interaction mirrors what happens within the Productivity Circle. These connections are generally quite purposeful and are sustained by a clearly expressed mutual interest. Examples include book clubs, groups of music fans, automotive forums, and even alumni pages. A common topic helps structure the interaction and ensures that everyone can find something interesting to discuss. A participant's activity may decline over time, but it is always easy for them to return to the group. Such groups rarely sustain extremely close relationships, but they are a good way to establish regular interaction with other members. You may not even know many members of an online group personally, but they can become a valuable and reliable source of new connections and useful information.

One example of the skillful use of subject-specific channels online is the case of Dickson Yeo, a doctoral candidate from Singapore who was apparently recruited by Chinese intelligence. He used his extensive LinkedIn connections to collect information from people with ties to military and government officials in the United States. At trial, Yeo admitted that he had contacted and negotiated with high-ranking officials, using a fake consulting company to post job listings and obtain the information he needed. Yeo maintained a five-year dialogue with one of his many contacts, a U.S. Air Force security specialist, first through LinkedIn and then by phone, periodically requesting various consulting services.

Despite ultimately being detained, Yeo provides a good example of how ubiquitous and effective online networks have become. Intelligence services can take full advantage of online space, not least because they know that, amid the millions of business contacts taking place online every minute, they can recruit new sources secretly and without interference.

The third and least frequent form of online interaction involves contacts you may have "frozen" for a time for one reason or another, whether because of the great distance between you or because there is no immediate need to communicate. These contacts may once have been in your Productivity Circle but gradually moved to its outer edge. You get in touch with them no more than a couple of times a year. We recommend making a list of the people with whom you would like to remain in contact and setting several "reminders" in your calendar so that you reach out on time and remind them of yourself. It is useful to keep notes on what you discussed last time as a way of demonstrating your care and interest in the other person. Fortunately, chat logs make this easier. Interaction at this level usually takes the form of a video or phone call or a long electronic message in which you can discuss important events that have occurred since your last contact.

Before the whole world plunged headlong into the internet, people used greeting cards for this purpose, exchanging them during the holidays and reporting the latest important events in their lives. Our goal is to keep your contacts from "drifting away" for good. They should remain close enough that you would not feel awkward asking them a favor out of the blue. Try speaking openly about personal matters or current problems, and you will be surprised by how readily they reciprocate. To compensate for infrequent communication, you need to give your messages greater "depth" and make them as open and personal as possible, if not "philosophical."

The simplest thing you can do online or by phone is remind your friends that you are aware of important events in their lives, such as their birthdays or their children's birthdays. Modern technology makes significant dates easy to track, but you must pick up the phone or write the message yourself. No one will do it for you. Facebook birthday reminders can be a good first step, but to go a little further, you should remember other important events in the lives of the people around you. This will help you stand out in other people's minds. For a serious networker, every such date is an opportunity to draw people closer. If you diligently record facts about your contacts' family and professional lives, along with the wishes and preferences they share with you, you will accumulate a wealth of useful assets that can be used to create and reinforce a positive attitude toward you.

In the virtual world, the icon bearing your photograph on various sites is the only image by which other people judge you. That is why you should think carefully about creating the right social-media profile. If people find it difficult to discern your human qualities or understand what you like and dislike, or if your profile seems contrived and fake, they will be reluctant to make contact. You will then miss the opportunity to move relationships from the virtual world into the real one.

Online communication is easy to abuse. With a single click, your message is sent to hundreds of people. It may seem original and instructive to you, but your friends may have received the same thing before. We are all inundated with tired jokes, countless photographs of kittens, and copies of email exchanges between people we do not even know. To keep your contacts from unsubscribing or deleting these messages as a waste of time, send your online friends only things they genuinely need and find interesting. Do not forget the wise advice: treat others as you would like them to treat you!

The challenge of personal "marketing" now looms over everyone. One need only look at an "influencer's" online profile to understand how effective personal branding can be. We recommend focusing your efforts on making your profile attractive to the people you want to draw in. Do not try to please everyone. Given the enormous scale of the online world, we strongly recommend a more targeted approach. Once you have established yourself and your image, work on moving relationships from the Development Circle to the Productivity Circle. You will soon discover that your digital life has become a useful extension of your everyday life. It will serve as a kind of multiplier for your usual networking efforts.

Chapter 5. Methods for Strengthening Relationships

Our discussion of building relationships now moves from general strategic principles to tactics and tools. What can you do to strengthen and deepen connections? As the person with the primary interest in these relationships, you need to take the initiative and determine what should happen, where, and how. You are responsible for the actions that will bring you closer to other people. Below, we offer several methods for strengthening relationships.

The essence of relationship development lies in the gradual removal of barriers between people. These barriers include differences in emotional state, social position, lifestyle, background, political views, and so on. As your connections strengthen and emotional involvement grows, such differences will have less and less effect on the stability of your relationships. If you want a relationship to develop, you need to demonstrate that no differences will affect your friendship. In most cases, you will not be asked to betray your convictions: if you remain true to yourself, people will respect you even more.

Open Up to Another Person

The first way to remove interpersonal barriers is to open up and talk about yourself. This usually helps the other person do the same, bringing the relationship to a new level of trust. Opening up, however, sometimes means exposing your doubts and weaknesses. Taking the first step involves risk, but the risk is worthwhile. We always want to show our best side and appear successful, so we generally hide our problems and failures. But everyone else does the same. When we put on a show of success, we make others envious and encourage competition, which destroys sincerity in a relationship. A touch of imperfection and vulnerability will not hurt you; instead, it will help lower the barriers between you. There is no point in trying to outdo the other person. Your goal is not to impress but, above all, to draw them to you!

Deep relationships necessarily involve openness about the areas that matter most to people: family, health, money, and children. If your interactions include communicating with family members, discussing hobbies, health, and finances, and debating the best ways to educate children, you are on the right path toward mutual trust. As soon as the relationship feels stable, therefore, take the initiative and steer some of your conversations toward these topics.

A good way to assess your progress in building a relationship of trust is to find out whether your contacts can talk about their weaknesses and failures. Like everyone else, we have plenty of these to share. When the time is right, try going first and see whether the other person reciprocates. Solving problems together significantly deepens a relationship. It also gives you an opportunity to help and, if that help is accepted, to make a similar request yourself later.

Expand the Sphere of Interaction

When discussing how to strengthen new relationships, we emphasized that bringing new contacts into your social environment, belonging to the same professional organization, or living in the same neighborhood can have a very positive effect. Mutual acquaintances and shared experiences - in short, seeing the world from the same perspective - create a sense of belonging and camaraderie. You cannot always move somewhere else or change professions to build a stronger relationship, however. Fortunately, there are other ways to achieve the same goal.

You can bring your spouse or partner - or even your children - into the relationship. Even if your acquaintance is single, interacting with you in a new, broader context will certainly be beneficial. You will see your contact in a new setting and a somewhat different light, eliciting new ways of interacting. This can reveal aspects of their personality that were hidden by the social roles you both adopted in earlier meetings. For example, watching someone in meetings and on business trips will not show you what kind of husband or father he is. In addition, your spouse or partner will give you a fresh pair of eyes and notice things that you might miss. For intelligence officers, a second pair of eyes offers an invaluable opportunity to assess the true degree of closeness and sincerity in a relationship. Introducing your contact to someone new from your inner circle also allows that person to ask questions or raise the same subjects from a different angle.

Consider a story from our work in the United States. One of us met a financial consultant of Chinese descent. He worked for a technology company that interested us. The man could have become a useful source of information, so we decided to try to build a closer relationship with him. For a long time, however, the relationship remained cold and formal. He ignored every attempt to move the conversation beyond business. Given his Chinese background, we suspected that building a stronger connection might take more time, but the complete lack of progress was concerning.

As a final attempt, we invited him and his wife to dinner. This gave both of us an excellent opportunity to observe the couple in a relaxed setting and try to understand the situation better. After a few drinks and an informal conversation between the women in the kitchen, it became clear that the wife played the leading role in this Chinese family. The husband looked to her for approval in almost everything. We decided that strengthening the relationship between the men would require strengthening the connection between their female "halves." We spent the next several months building rapport with his wife and gradually bringing the two families closer. Clearly, without an attentive pair of female eyes and teamwork, we would never have developed a deep relationship with this man.

When you bring a new person into your circle of friends, you indirectly bind them to you. They will form connections with people who like and trust you. Each of those people will project their positive image of you onto the newcomer, strengthening the new person's desire to trust you and grow closer. From that point on, breaking with you will effectively mean breaking with the entire group. And remember: everyone in your circle becomes an additional source of information about the new contact.

Provide Help and Support

Encouragement and support are among the most valuable "currencies" in a relationship. We never seem to have enough kind words and understanding: after almost forty years of marriage, we still complain that we do not receive enough of either from each other. A compliment is a powerful relationship-building "drug" because people yearn to hear something pleasant about themselves. A compliment, however, must never turn into flattery.

Mutual assistance is a useful building block of trust. If you know that a friend or colleague has a problem and may need help, do not hesitate to offer it. At worst, your offer will be politely declined, but the gesture itself will certainly be remembered!

In our intelligence work, we discovered an interesting paradox. Many people believe that asking for help is bound to harm a relationship because it makes them look weak and dependent. People dislike appearing weak or dependent and fear that doing so will bewilder or repel the other person. In reality, the opposite is true. By showing some vulnerability and asking for help, you put the other person in a position to demonstrate their strength and capabilities, thereby raising their status in their own eyes. There is one qualification, however: you should ask only for something your acquaintance can do without much effort. This is especially important in the early stages of a relationship, when the connection is still weak and any misunderstanding can easily break it. When people ask for and offer help to one another, their relationship grows closer. That is hardly surprising, since each person begins to play a more active role in the other's life.

As soon as someone offers you help, you become a kind of debtor. This gives you an opportunity to repay the person's kindness with interest. They may later say, "Oh, you shouldn't have!" At the same time, however, they will be pleased both that you remembered their kindness and that they benefited from it. From then on, they will consider you reliable, honest, and pleasant. This leads to a rule for requesting help that can serve as an effective relationship-building tool: ask for little, but repay kindness generously.

We suggest assessing your qualities, experience, and contacts and compiling a list - a kind of "catalog" of what you are willing to share. The catalog might include professionals you can recommend to others, such as a friend who is a chiropractor. Do you enjoy running, bird-watching, or origami? Share your skills and enthusiasm with someone. Even if none of this "hooks" anyone, you will come across as an interesting person and may be recommended to others. Do not be afraid to give more than you receive. If you make it your goal to help others become happier and more successful, you can be sure they will respond in kind.

Host Lunches and Parties

Here is an interesting fact: most meaningful conversations take place around a table. If we need to discuss something quickly, we say, "Let's get coffee." A serious business matter inevitably calls for a "business lunch"! To thank someone for a favor, we suggest having dinner together. In a relaxed setting - often after everyone has had a bit too much to drink - you encourage your companions to open up and grow closer.

That is why, as a networker, you need to develop your skills as a host of dinners and parties. Not all of us are natural conversationalists or "party people," nor were we at the beginning of our careers. Skill comes with experience, but there is always a solution. At first, invite a talkative friend who can serve as a kind of "master of ceremonies."

When people are deciding whether to attend a party or invent an excuse, the main question is always the same: who else will be there? An event's success depends on having the right mix of guests. The rules are fairly simple. First, most guests should know and like one another. This creates excitement and the necessary "warm-up." Second, you need a reasonable balance of men and women, married and single people, and locals and out-of-towners so that no one grows bored. Third, you can invite several new faces, but they should make up no more than one-quarter of the group. That ratio introduces a fresh dynamic while helping to avoid awkward silences. Fourth, it is useful to have a "star guest": an engaging storyteller, someone of high status, or simply a friend who has just returned from a trip to Tibet...

Create a Positive Atmosphere

Your positivity, openness, and confidence are essential foundations for a good relationship. The task is simple: create a light, positive atmosphere. People should enjoy your company. Any negativity or criticism immediately lowers the "temperature" of the interaction.

Praising or complimenting someone with whom you are building a relationship is one of the surest ways to create positive feelings toward you. We know how effectively flattery exploits the brain's need for positive "reinforcement." An intelligence officer must tread very carefully here, however. Intelligence officers focus on working with smart people, and a smart person will recognize insincere flattery even if, deep down, they enjoy it. As soon as you are caught using such crude tools, trust in the relationship will fall. For praise to be effective, it must be based on the person's real achievements, positive character traits, or actions. The same applies to compliments, whether they concern an attractive tie or how refreshed the person looks after a vacation.

Above all, you must be sincere and truthful. Do not pretend that nothing happened if, for example, you were involved in a traffic accident on the way to a meeting. Simply explain what happened and reschedule the meeting. You may even receive the bonus of sympathy and an offer of help, which is itself one of the building blocks of a relationship.

Help Others Get Talking

The atmosphere in which a relationship develops depends on how well the two people connect emotionally. That cannot happen unless you draw people into conversation. Conversely, when a friend begins to talk, they share their emotions, and you become part of their emotional world. Remember that, as an active listener, you are the driving force of the conversation.

You can guide the conversation in the desired direction, provided that your gestures and posture also express genuine interest. With the right questions, you can shift its focus without making the other person feel that they are being steered toward a particular destination. To them, the conversation will seem to unfold naturally. Guiding a conversation with questions is like redirecting the flow of water by digging channels or building dams.

By moving the conversation from one subject to another, you have a better chance of learning about the other person than if you asked directly. Direct questions, especially those that arise outside the context of the conversation, will most likely be ignored or rebuffed. Our advice, then, is to train yourself not to ask direct questions at all, including so-called "closed questions" that can be answered with a simple "yes" or "no." Open questions should begin with a phrase such as "How do you feel about...?" This allows the person to frame the answer as they wish, without placing boundaries on their thinking. Giving the speaker that freedom can teach you more about their interests and motivations.

Do Something Interesting Together

Once you understand someone's priorities and interests, you can use one of the most effective tools for building a relationship: spending time together doing something you both enjoy. Nothing reveals another person quite like traveling together and sharing new experiences. You do not need to watch the latest Hollywood buddy-cop movie to understand that. We already know that the most heartfelt conversations happen while fishing or at our children's soccer games.

Several years ago, an acquaintance called and invited me to go fishing. To be honest, fishing is not my thing, and we were not especially close. Moreover, I suspected that, after calling me out of the blue following several months of silence, he expected some kind of favor. My first impulse was to decline, but I had no better plans for the weekend, so I agreed in the hope that some time outdoors would take my mind off the city's summer heat. By the time we reached the fishing spot, the weather had deteriorated: clouds had rolled in and a strong wind was blowing. By evening, thunder had begun to rumble and rain was falling. In short, the fishing trip was a failure. When the rain stopped that evening, we were soaked through and built a fire to dry our clothes. To warm ourselves further, we opened a bottle of whiskey and spent the next several hours talking - first about our misadventures with the weather, and then about almost everything: our lives, family problems, and children. Was it the best outing of my life? Certainly not. It was, however, unforgettable. The new impressions and shared experience strengthened our relationship in a single bound, almost overnight.

How to Plan Productive Meetings

An intelligence officer's task is not merely to build relationships, but to build them quickly and effectively. That is why an intelligence officer must get the most out of every interaction. Every meeting an operative conducts must be carefully planned and prepared, sometimes down to the smallest detail. If you want to achieve better results in your interactions with people, you can adopt the same approach used by members of our profession.

At every meeting, you pursue two objectives at once: advancing the relationship and deepening your understanding of the other person. Developing the relationship and improving mutual understanding are really two sides of the same coin, each reinforcing the other. As you prepare for a meeting, you must determine exactly how you will achieve both objectives.

Developing productive exchanges is the best way to strengthen a connection. This process of "give and take" is constantly taking place between people, consciously or unconsciously. Making those exchanges more deliberate and purposeful can significantly improve the relationship. Before every meeting, therefore, think through what you can offer and what you can ask for.

Finally, you need to plan the next steps in the relationship. After all, you want your future meetings to take place under better conditions - and produce better results!

Diagnosing Problems in Relationships

Relationships do not develop in a straight line. Any relationship can encounter obstacles. Even with the best intentions, you will not be able to avoid every pitfall or defuse every mine. The radar you used to scan people at your first meeting, the "OIS" sorting principle (the source-language acronym for "Dangerous? Interesting? Difficult?"), should never be switched off. In some cases, the other person will end the relationship; in others, you will stop communicating because there has been no progress and you have found no common ground. To avoid serious problems, we must act like doctors. We need to diagnose problems in relationships honestly as they arise. That is why your "virtual GoPro camera" must always be running, so that you can detect rough spots or flaws in communication in time.

One warning sign is a change in your contact's commitment to developing the relationship. This may take the form of trying to limit interaction by reducing its intensity: repeatedly rescheduling meetings or failing to answer calls or messages. From time to time, you hear excuses and promises to get in touch, but the overall pattern remains the same. We usually hope such problems are temporary, but most often they are not. If you have done nothing to cause it, the other person's behavior means that communicating with you is not a priority for them. Rather than let the problem drag on, we suggest discussing it directly with your contact. You have nothing to lose by asking a sincere question, and the answer may reveal a mistake on your part that you had not recognized before. If so, you may be able to correct it.

Another warning sign is a lack of initiative in your interactions. At a more advanced stage of a relationship, reciprocity can serve as a kind of litmus test. At the outset, some passivity on the other person's part may be understandable. But if the initiative always comes from you and the other person never shows any desire to call first, something has gone wrong. In our operational experience, we had a case that we discussed at length. One of our potentially interesting contacts never took the initiative, and we practically had to drag him to meetings. Yet when we met, he was very talkative and enthusiastic. He clearly enjoyed these conversations, which went on for many hours, but would then vanish without a trace for several weeks. After long debate, we concluded that he was simply bored and was using us to entertain himself. We decided to stop calling him, and we proved to be right: he never contacted us again.

If someone who had shown no desire to develop the relationship suddenly tries to revive it, it is quite likely that there is something about them you do not know. At best, they may want to "sell" you something. For intelligence officers, such sudden interest inevitably arouses suspicion: there is a serious risk that someone has sent this person to you. That is exactly what happened in our case...

Besides these two signs of impending problems in a relationship, you must also monitor the level of emotional involvement and the growth of mutual openness and trust. If, despite all your efforts, someone does not want to open up, your personalities may clash and the person may never come to like you. They may seem polite and even encourage you at every opportunity, but the relationship will still reach a dead end. For example, if an American never invites you home, there is no trace of trust. Europeans, incidentally, may have a valid reason for avoiding this: many apartments in old European cities are simply too small to accommodate guests comfortably.

One way to test whether a relationship has a future is to describe a problem that concerns you and ask for help. As we mentioned earlier, you should ask only for something the person can do without devoting much time or many resources to it. Their reaction will usually show their true character.

It is difficult, then, to judge the "health" of a relationship from any single interaction, whether pleasant or less so. Relationships depend on many factors. They may improve, deteriorate, or stall. Nevertheless, if you monitor their dynamics carefully and watch for warning signs, you will probably be able to revive most troubled relationships.

As you analyze the dynamics of your interactions with other people, ask yourself where your relationships currently stand, what problems and opportunities you face, and what your "flight plan" should be for the next period. Assess your connections with each key contact: are you comfortable with each other, and are you living up to each other's expectations? Are these relationships even still alive? Just as a military surgeon faced with a flood of gravely wounded patients must decide whom to operate on first and who is, in effect, doomed to die, an intelligence officer, politician, or sales manager must decide which relationships must be strengthened at any cost and which should be abandoned.

Chapter 6. "Penetration" into Organizations

Economists distinguish between microeconomics, which studies the behavior of individual firms, and macroeconomics, which deals with the functioning of the economy as a whole. Using this distinction, we can similarly divide networking into micro- and macro-networking. Micro-networking focuses on developing connections with a particular person, from the first contact to close friendship. Macro-networking concerns managing connections with an entire organization and strengthening one's position in society. Whether you are an employee working with a client company, a manager appointed to a new department, or someone planning a career in politics, it is extremely important to become an insider in an organization or raise your status within the group with which you work.

Organization is a very broad concept. It encompasses everything from a military unit to a volunteer foreign-language study group. In an organization or any structured group, your relationship with one person cannot be entirely separated from your relationships with everyone else. It may be an exaggeration to say that an organization acts as a single team; nevertheless, it has its own rules of internal interaction, which we can neither ignore nor change.

"Deciphering" Organizations

The chief distinguishing feature among organizations is their degree of hierarchy. In vertical organizations, hierarchy is absolute: a higher position automatically gives you power over every employee below you, as well as better access to information, which flows predominantly from the top. The opposite of a hierarchical organization is a "horizontal" one. In its extreme form, it is a completely unstructured group in which every member is equal. In reality, the structures of nearly all organizations you encounter are mixed: they combine vertical and horizontal elements.

The pattern of relationships within an organization depends on the formal and informal power of the people in it. Formal power consists of the authority conferred by a person's official position, their role in carrying out the organization's tasks, their place in key organizational processes, and their access to resources. Informal power, by contrast, combines a person's will and charisma, competence, physical attractiveness, and, to a substantial degree, the capabilities of their personal network of contacts.

In an organization, three different sets of relationships interact: bureaucratic, power-based, and personal. Bureaucratic relationships reflect the official management structure. For example, someone who officially heads a department can award a bonus or issue a reprimand, thereby compelling subordinates to meet certain requirements. Power relationships are only indirectly tied to such formal structures. They reflect strength of character, experience, personal authority, and social connections: a person's ability to influence others. Many managers, by virtue of their age and experience, "radiate" more power than their position confers. Power relationships also exist between senior and junior team members who are formally at the same level. Personal relationships arise from the human feelings between people in the organization: love and respect, hatred and fear, friendship and competition.

Because we are focused on relationships between people, questions of power and management are crucial. We need to understand how organizations really function, regardless of what their official documents say. That is precisely why we must distinguish the formal power structures and decision-making processes described on paper from the real ones. Official management structures often fail to reflect the real distribution of power within organizations, whether global business corporations or local charitable foundations. For example, a charitable organization may actually be led by the wife of a business magnate, who formally holds the position of deputy director for events, while its official head, a former minister, appears in public only a couple of times a year.

Without understanding its real power dynamics, you cannot work effectively with any organization; you will simply allow people to pull the wool over your eyes. Here is the essence of our advice: assume nothing about an organization's power structure, and treat it as a "black box" until you have studied it carefully.

Behind the formal structures of organizations are tacit power groups that compete for decision-making authority and control over staffing policy and budgets. Their strength rests on the networks of personal connections among group members and on the influence of their leaders. It also depends on the group's ability to unite against others, gain control of information and resources, and use members' personal relationships to the fullest.

It is important to note that power in organizations belongs to groups, not individuals. Mutual trust among group members and their collective control over the organization's vital functions enable them to preserve their common power. A person who tries to control an organization alone, even from the very top, is always at risk. A group of subordinates can unite to curb an "overreaching" boss and wrest more control for itself. Most often, the boss either follows a policy of "divide and rule," setting groups of subordinates against one another, or personally heads the dominant group.

How to Draw an Organization Map

An organization map can serve as a lifeline, keeping you from drowning in the complexity of its power dynamics. Such a map will differ significantly from the formal organizational chart because it shows not just functional structure but actual power relationships. Its basic graphical elements, people and connections, are similar to those used on your personal network map; however, it should place special emphasis on functional and power groups.

Because of the internal complexity of most businesses and political organizations, representing them as two-dimensional graphs is not easy. Any picture we draw can only be a rough approximation of the multidimensional relationships that exist in reality. As with your personal network, an adequate picture of the organization will not emerge immediately, but only after several unsuccessful attempts to capture and simplify it. Do not despair: without such a picture, understanding what is happening is even harder! So, as before, let us work through the process one step at a time.

Stage 1

At the center of a blank sheet of paper, draw a small circle. It will represent the organization's center of power. Later, place several dots or small circles inside it to represent the people who make key decisions. Sometimes it may seem that only one person dominates the organization and "deserves" a place in the central circle. If so, so be it. Around the central circle, draw another circle with a larger radius. It will hold other, less important "bigwigs." Finally, draw an outer circle marking the boundary of the organization; everyone else will be placed within it.

Stage 2

From the center of your drawing, extend lines to the outer circle, dividing the organization into key sectors: business units or functional divisions such as finance, marketing, and research and development. Each sector can in turn be divided into its component parts.

Stage 3

Use dots or small circles to mark the key figures in the organization according to their positions and functions. For example, place the head of finance in the finance sector; depending on their actual power, however, they may or may not belong in the central circle of "bigwigs." Once you have placed all the key figures on the map, connect them with lines or arrows showing the direction, quality, and intensity of their relationships, just as on your personal network map.

Stage 4

Finally, as a last but certainly no less important step, identify the competing power groups in the organization. These often semi-secret alliances can be discerned, at least in part, by carefully analyzing the relationships among key decision-makers. This closeness is indicated not by the direction or intensity of the relationships, but rather by their quality. The most reliable information about who is on whose team will come from your sources who are themselves company insiders. To show which power group each person belongs to, color the people differently or draw colored lines around the groups.

Once you have a map, use it to develop your strategy. As with personal networks, find out which of your contacts are close to the real "bigwigs," and who could become your "connectors," information sources, "gatekeepers," and "bridges."

How to Pave a Path into an Organization

A thoughtful, strategic approach matters even in a relationship with one individual; when dealing with organizations, such complex nodes of human interconnection, it becomes crucial. Ask yourself what you want from a given organization or company and who can give it to you. Do you want to become an exclusive supplier? Do you want the organization to hire you? Do you ultimately want it to form a strategic alliance with your business? Or perhaps you want it to contribute to your charitable foundation?

Our goal is to offer recommendations that will help you build the necessary relationships within an organization while making fewer mistakes. They may seem intuitive, but putting them into practice properly will require some experience and persistence. Creating a network map that shows who is who in the organization will help you plan your approach carefully. As you progress, the map will help you retain your growing knowledge of key "nodes" and centers of power, enabling you later to chart paths to the right people.

Using the networking terms you already know, you will most likely enter the organization through a "bridge": someone you met at a conference or through a friend of a friend. Your first and very urgent priority, however, is to find a second, independent "bridge" into the company. Not every relationship will work as intended. Your relationship with the person who brought you in may break down too, closing that imaginary "door." Moreover, this person may well want to control your relationships within their organization or even derive some benefit from doing so. If you build stronger relationships with someone else, they will most likely react with jealousy and displeasure. After all, in a certain sense, every other employee in the organization is a competitor to them.

Clearly, finding an additional "bridge" will take time. At first, you simply do not have enough information about the organization. Your second priority, therefore, is to use your initial contact to reach "capacitors": true insiders who know how the organization functions and how power is really distributed. We hope they can give you enough information to help you avoid becoming embroiled in conflicts among competing groups. If you are perceived as a tool of one of them, this will greatly limit your future opportunities.

Although finding additional "bridges" is a priority at the outset, this does not prevent you from drawing on other people you meet inside the organization. The basic principles of network development still apply, including the search for "connectors," "capacitors," and "conductors." Here, "conductors" are people who can lead you to particular parts of the company - the procurement department, the research and development department, or company divisions in other cities.

Not all of an organization's employees are equally important to achieving your goals. Your ultimate objective is to reach the people who influence the decisions that matter to you. Once you have established your initial contacts and secured a presence in the company, your next step is to identify the real centers of power and find influential figures. These people are not necessarily at the top of the organizational chart. Moreover, direct contact with the most senior decision-makers can often create problems. You are likely to come under attack from everyone else who is also competing for their time and attention.

What criteria can you use to identify the people you need in order to strengthen your influence within an organization? They should:

  • exercise clear influence over decision-making in the organization;
  • be "insiders" with direct ties to the principal power groups;
  • have the potential to rise further within the organization;
  • be willing to develop a close personal relationship with you.

The last point is extremely important: if your personalities are incompatible, if the person does not like you, or if they do not listen to your advice, then despite all the power they possess, the most you will be able to do is use them as an occasional source of information.

When looking for influential figures, you should make every effort to cultivate "capacitors" who can serve as your personal "guides." Such people retain the entire history of the organization's development. They remember who made good and bad decisions, who carried out prestigious or failed projects, who sided with whom, and why particular individuals ended up in the most senior leadership positions. The more such "guides" you have, the better you will understand what is really happening and where the organization is headed.

Once you understand how things really work, you may have an opportunity to become an information broker and influencer within the company yourself. I once met several managers responsible for major corporate accounts who served as reliable conduits for information and new ideas inside their client organizations. They claimed to know those organizations even better than the clients themselves did. This should not be surprising: companies often have many internal barriers and taboos surrounding communication, and only outsiders can breach them. Once an organization's employees see you as a channel for conveying important messages across the company or up to its leadership, you can say that you have truly achieved your objective and become an insider.

From our experience at an international business consulting firm that served as "cover" for intelligence activities, we recall several "working group" sessions designed to help multinational semiconductor companies increase chip sales to the largest smartphone manufacturers. For these projects, the two sides assembled global teams that included representatives from research and development, supply-chain management, manufacturing, and other business units involved in launching new products. Watching the seller's team leader - a cheerful, good-natured Italian who managed the semiconductor company's international accounts - it was impossible to tell whose side he was on, the seller's or the buyer's. During negotiations, he sat on the client's side. In discussions with the client's employees, he talked about details of product-development programs of which, judging by their surprised reactions, even prominent members of the client team were unaware. Moreover, this account manager later admitted that the client company's leadership regularly turned to him for help recruiting new employees...

Decision-Making Styles

You may want an organization to make certain decisions that benefit you and your business. The possible strategies for building contacts and strengthening your influence, however, will depend heavily on the organization's decision-making style.

Decision-making styles depend on the balance of power within an organization and on the influence that different groups exert over the appointment of managers or the selection of suppliers. Some parts of a company may come under the influence of competing stakeholder groups. For example, one group may be able to block certain strategic decisions in practice through a key appointee who serves as vice president of finance. Similarly, groups may keep one another in check by placing their own people on important decision-making bodies, such as a procurement committee.

Regardless of the mechanisms of power at work or the level of management involved, organizational decision-making generally falls into one of three styles. The first centers on a principal decision-maker, sometimes even someone with a monopoly on decision-making - a "czar." To prevail, it is usually enough to persuade this "czar" to personally support, or lobby for, the decision you want. Obviously, in this case your organizational strategy should focus on gaining access to this key figure and influencing them.

Under the second style, decisions are made by a committee composed of representatives of different functions and, very often, competing power groups. Persuading one or more members of such a committee, even its most senior and influential members, is unlikely to achieve much if others oppose your proposal. Here, unlike in the case of the "czar," your aim is not so much to recruit active supporters as to avoid making enemies. To accomplish this, every member of the committee must see you as independent, impartial, and unaffiliated with any of the competing groups.

The third, "bureaucratic," style applies when there is no single, clearly defined decision-making authority, whether a "king" or a "committee." A decision emerges from a lengthy bureaucratic process in which every proposal is evaluated in stages against different criteria; in other words, practically no one assumes personal responsibility for the decision as a whole. This nightmarish scenario is increasingly common in large companies and government organizations, and it demands enormous amounts of time, effort, and patience from everyone involved. From a networking perspective, it is the most difficult case because it requires detailed knowledge of both the organization's processes and the people along the decision-making path. It is hardly surprising that only a few "privileged" suppliers can accumulate the extensive library of personal contacts and organizational experience required to keep doing business with such a client.

For example, when it comes to government or military procurement in the United States, very few contractors today can understand - let alone comply with - all the details in tender documents that enumerate every congressional requirement. An average company's only chance of getting a piece of this lucrative "pie" is therefore to become a subcontractor to giants such as Boeing or Booz Allen Hamilton, which take a hefty cut from contractors while retaining control over the clients. And how does Boeing know how to meet its clients' needs? Very simply: by hiring former generals - Pentagon insiders!

How to Secure a Place in a Leader's Mind

Now is the time to discuss another important skill of a good networker: understanding the mindset and needs of the decision-maker you want to reach and using the appropriate language. In other words, you must be on the same wavelength, speak the same language, and see the world through that decision-maker's eyes.

In our experience, your chances of establishing a relationship will inevitably disappear if you misread decision-makers' mentality and needs, fail to align yourself with their priorities, and consequently frame what you want from them in the wrong way. The reasons are simple: decision-makers are constantly bombarded by people demanding their attention, and they do not want to waste their time. To survive the onslaught of work and visitors, they have learned to treat all incoming requests like tennis balls - reflexively bouncing as many of them as possible to other people and keeping only the few that interest them personally. They become interested when information that reaches their desks has the potential to benefit them personally, whether materially, professionally, or in some other way.

In business, this works roughly as follows. Imagine that you are a sales manager at an IT systems integrator. At last, you secure a meeting with the head of international logistics at a large corporation. How would you position yourself in order to build a productive relationship?

You could talk about upgrading the inventory-management system. In that case, your target would very likely "bounce the ball" down to a subordinate - the director of information technology. Or you could propose a detailed audit of the company's IT systems to introduce best practices. On hearing that proposal, your prospective partner would most likely bounce the "ball" higher within the organization, saying that the matter should be addressed to the corporation's chief operating officer. In either case, your chances of continuing the conversation with the head of international logistics and establishing a direct working relationship would become negligible.

To remain at the level of the decision-maker with whom you want to develop a relationship, rather than being "kicked" upward or downward, you need to understand how you can help solve the particular problems that manager faces. You are unlikely to learn this without help from corporate insiders. Imagine, for example, that you discover that the head of international logistics is fighting the company's regional branches, which have long wanted the freedom to select their own information technology suppliers. In that situation, a timely proposal to implement new security features throughout the organization would help your target in the struggle with those rivals by providing strong arguments for retaining control over the branches' decisions. At the same time, it would earn you a place as a valued ally and even a friend.

Setting Up Channels of Influence

We hope that your plans for an organization are not limited to immediate concerns. There is no point in spending months, much less years, of your valuable time developing relationships if you have no long-term plan to influence future corporate decisions. If you do have such plans, however, you will need to build special relationships with two types of people within the organization.

The first type consists of the "visionaries" who shape the company's future. They can usually be found among people such as the chief technology officer, the head of marketing, or a new-product manager. Each in their own capacity, and all of them together, determine the direction in which the organization will move.

The other type consists of "rising stars." These are people who will most likely be in power tomorrow but are still accessible today. They are not yet surrounded by a "cordon sanitaire" of deputies and assistants, nor have they yet become targets for their detractors. Dynamic and ambitious, such people are usually quite open to new ideas, opportunities, and contacts, as our own experience has shown.

There is another characteristic common to both types that may prove useful to you. Both "visionaries" and "rising stars" are highly mobile and constantly seek opportunities to develop the business and advance their own careers. Because they have competitors and enemies within the company and frequently communicate with headhunters, they prefer to keep any interaction with outsiders away from their own offices. They are more likely than others to attend conferences and business education programs, where you may have an opportunity to cross paths with them and make contact in a more relaxed setting.

One of the most successful investments in recent years was Yahoo's decision to invest in Alibaba, then a young Chinese e-commerce platform. Much of this success was due to Yahoo co-founder Jerry Yang's ability to identify "rising stars." Yang was born in Taiwan but emigrated to the United States with his family at the age of ten. After studying at Stanford University, he founded an internet business that later became Yahoo, one of the most successful internet portals.

Three years after founding his business, Jerry made his first trip to mainland China. On arrival, he was met by Jack Ma, then a junior employee of the Chinese Ministry of Economy. The two grew close during a mountain hike, enthusiastically discussing the internet's potential. Inspired, Jack Ma founded Alibaba in the hope of using the internet to bring Chinese companies onto the world stage.

In 2005, Jerry and Jack met again at a summit for leaders from China and Silicon Valley. Alibaba was gaining momentum in China but remained an outsider in the global market. The perceptive Jerry correctly assessed Jack Ma's potential and, during a short walk along the beach, negotiated an investment in the then-unprofitable Alibaba. The deal later proved momentous. Yahoo's investment in Alibaba paid off handsomely (fortyfold!), which only underscores Jerry Yang's remarkable skill at identifying talented partners.

Four Problems in Working with Organizations

When working with organizations, you will most likely encounter four typical problems. The first is managing your priorities and your time. Unless you focus with laser precision on what matters most, you will be drawn into the company's internal squabbles or become bogged down in relationships with people who are kind and pleasant but have no real power, ultimately failing to achieve the desired results. You will have to make difficult decisions about whom you spend time with, how much time you give them, and which relationships should be "frozen." Once again, a map of the organization's contact network will help you stay on course.

The second difficulty arises when you suddenly lose important supporters inside the company. People do not stay in the same positions forever. They move away, fall into conflict with others, or get fired... And they will not warn you beforehand. As a result, you lose access and sources of information, and your time and effort go to waste. This is why you must never rely on just one person. Continually developing an entire network of supporters should be a constant priority.

One of us lost access to a large organization in France when its first vice president - an active supporter and good friend - came into conflict with a major shareholder who, after many years as a passive investor, decided to take control. We had worked with the organization for many years, but despite that long history, every door abruptly closed to us. Could we have foreseen such an unexpected turn of events? As networking strategists, we probably could have. Had we followed the simple rule of not concentrating everything on one person, we would not have faced this problem.

The third difficulty is overcoming dependence and jealousy. People, especially those who brought you into the organization and helped you at first, want to control your progress within the company: they want to know whom you are meeting and what new things you are learning. When you need to move on, expanding your influence and building other relationships at higher levels, your former helpers may grow jealous and, if you fail to handle them properly, may even obstruct you. Managing such relationships is a delicate matter that requires great tact and, unfortunately, time. The least you can do to minimize this jealousy is chart your course through the company so that your original "guides" introduce you to the new contacts themselves.

In the unfortunate case described above involving the first vice president, when all the "eggs," as the saying goes, had been placed in one basket, not one but two mistakes were made. Why did the organization's other senior managers fail to come to our aid even though they had been perfectly satisfied with our work together? Because they envied the consultant's "exclusive" relationship with their former boss. Had enough been done to strengthen relationships with several executives rather than just one? Clearly not...

The fourth problem arises if you become entangled in a power struggle within an organization where you work or with which you do business. Managers fighting for power commonly enlist outside experts - consultants, lawyers, and supplier representatives - as tools for pursuing their personal objectives. Such "mercenaries," or rather, "cannon fodder," will no longer be needed once the task has been completed. There are even cases in which warring factions hire a "temporary" boss to implement policies that could damage their own reputations. Carlos Ghosn, a Frenchman of Lebanese and Brazilian descent who rose to fame as CEO of Renault, was hired by the owners of Japan's Nissan to lead a painful restructuring that no Japanese executive would take on. As soon as the job was done, however, the real "bosses" of Japanese business behind Nissan, one of the symbols of Japanese industry, shamelessly got rid of him. This leads to an important lesson: never take sides in an organization's internal power struggles, even if you believe you are backing the winner. It may seem that you have won, but you may still need the cooperation of many of those who have just lost, and they can undoubtedly make your life very difficult later. This strategic mistake almost cost Carlos Ghosn many years in a Japanese prison; he was spared only by his daring escape to his homeland, Lebanon.

Influence in Networked and Virtual Organizations

What should you do if an organization has no center of power? What if it has no structure at all? Increasingly, we see organizations with very shallow formal hierarchies, from high-tech startups to virtual volunteer groups. They function like distributed networks. Yet even there, not everyone is equal. Who should you focus on when developing relationships? How can you identify centers of power in nonhierarchical organizations? How can you increase your influence there? Let us try to answer these questions.

In an ideal hierarchical organization, such as the military, your power is a function of your "vertical height" in the organizational structure. All power is delegated from above. There is only one way to increase your "weight": climb the organizational ladder.

But what if the organization is not merely networked but entirely virtual, for example, an online community in the IT or creative industries? How can you build your influence there? To describe the distribution of power in networks, let us use a metaphor. When you look at the sky on a clear night, the first things you notice are familiar stars, stars that look larger because they are closer to Earth, stars of extraordinary brightness, so-called "shooting" stars that suddenly cross the sky, and, finally, large constellations.

The same principles apply in the virtual world. To wield influence, and therefore a certain amount of power, in a personal network, you must either be well known, belong to someone's inner circle, establish yourself as a remarkable and vibrant personality, do something unusual, or belong to a well-known and respected group. If you want to become more visible and more important in the network, consider how to place yourself in one of these categories.

To become visible and well known, you need to act as a kind of "driver" of interactions and discussions, generate traffic around yourself, and take charge of a node on the communication platform. You need to become a virtual "connector."

If you want to get closer to specific people, you need to offer them something of value. You can find and share information that interests them, take part in their discussions, and help solve their problems. This will give you a reason to contact them directly.

But is it easy to become a "bright star"? If you are not confident in the value of your own ideas, you can become a skilled aggregator of other people's ideas. Become the "king of content," someone others can turn to for useful information. Create a kind of knowledge-exchange platform. People who lack time for detailed, lengthy searches will come straight to you. Better still, build a reputation as a source of "great material." This is precisely the role bloggers play, and their audiences often number in the millions.

Finally, a word about recognizable "constellations." As in real life, try to interact with well-known, interesting people. By participating in their chats and commenting on their posts, you will come to be associated with their circle, attract attention, and gain recognition in the online communities that interest you. In short, you will gain status "by association."

Chapter 7. Up the Social Ladder

For an intelligence officer, building a network of contacts is not the ultimate goal. It is only the necessary foundation and tool for accomplishing a more important objective. Intelligence officers must continually raise their social status and climb the social ladder. If they fail to do so, their value remains quite limited. People who have access to secrets and take part in important decisions sit at the top of society, whether they are politicians, government officials, or leading scientists. If they do not find you interesting, if you do not belong to the same social strata, if they cannot benefit from your knowledge and contacts, or if they do not find your personality engaging, they will have no desire to deal with you.

Social Capital

Continually climbing the social ladder has another name: accumulating personal social capital. Without delving into social-science terminology, social capital is the goodwill, recognition, and help you receive from people you know in the community around you. The accumulation of positive interactions with those around you gives you the right to the privileges of membership in the community and all its possible benefits, both material and intangible. Put simply, social capital is the "who you know" in the old saying, "It's not what you know, it's who you know."

Networking creates social capital. The amount of social capital, or reputation, you possess is a function of your personal and professional value multiplied by your success in networking. Your place in society is, in essence, your accumulated social capital. In business terms, your social status is your personal "capital," while the social capital you accumulate each year is your "profit." Your networking results are your "income." The stronger your personal network of contacts, the stronger your position in the relevant circles.

The value of strategy in networking becomes most obvious when you consider that your effectiveness, the ratio of the results achieved in strengthening your status to the effort expended, depends on how well you have set your priorities.

For illustration, let us quote American career coach Bonnie Marcus from Forbes magazine: "A strong personal network is like money in the bank. Your network can help you become visible, connect you with influential people, and open doors to new opportunities. Building and developing a network is one of the most powerful things you can do to support your own career growth. Nevertheless, our networking efforts often amount to little more than empty small talk, lack a systematic approach and, consequently, are ineffective. You make friends and connections, but those people are not always able to help you advance your career or, most importantly, put in a good word for you at the right moment." The quotation may seem long, but it shows how important a solid networking strategy is...

How to Turn Contacts into Social Capital

Methods for building social capital and social status can also be divided into micro and macro approaches. As discussed earlier, the micro approach is aimed at increasing your influence over the people around you, while the macro approach concerns your position in society as a whole. At the micro level, there are three proven ways to increase your social capital by drawing on your network of contacts. We have mentioned some of them before, but they are worth repeating here.

First, draw closer to centers of communication, to "connectors." Whether you are dealing with a company, a local community, or any other organization, try to become a kind of "exchange manager," or universal intermediary, and thus place yourself at the central node of all its relationships. The more central and visible your position, the better. Proactivity and volunteering are keys to success. On the one hand, being at the center of communications will help you "build" the necessary bridges and expand your network's reach. On the other, such a position will give you opportunities to do many good deeds: share information, show care and concern, offer help, and redirect resources. The next step is to become indispensable, someone who knows everyone and helps everyone. The payoff is worth the effort. The respect, gratitude, access to information, and new opportunities you acquire in the process will more than cover all your "operating costs."

Surprisingly, "connector" roles in many groups and organizations remain virtually unfilled. Perhaps this is because most people are too lazy or afraid to take the initiative and assume responsibility for communication flows. It is true that this work takes a great deal of time and effort. When one of us volunteered to revive the overseas alumni network of Harvard's John F. Kennedy School of Government, he immediately realized that no one had ever done so before; at least, the school had no official record of such an effort. If the "connector" role attracted so few people in such a famous institution, what can be said of other groups and communities?

Second, try to create your own "advisory board" and fill it with people who command respect and universal admiration. This need not be a formal group, and your advisors do not have to know one another personally. What matters is that these people can contribute positively to your personal growth or help your career by providing advice and connections.

The term "advisory board" may sound overly "corporate," but the practice of surrounding yourself with experienced, knowledgeable people is universal. Several years ago, after the first lecture in a course taught by one of this book's authors at MGIMO in Moscow, a student approached him and offered to serve as his course assistant. Over the semester, he had several lengthy conversations with her that covered subjects far beyond the lectures; the author shared his experience and effectively served as a coach. At the end of the course, he agreed to advise the student's thesis and later gave her a good recommendation when she applied for a job. Most remarkably, as it later turned out, the young woman had offered her help to professors in almost every course she took. Imagine how much advice, assistance, and how many valuable connections she gained during her years of study! We are confident that she will become a skilled diplomat and an excellent networker.

It is very important to include "rising stars" among your advisors and mentors, people who can pull you along as their own status rises. For an intelligence officer, identifying "rising stars" is one of the most important objectives. Such proactive work pays off handsomely. Consider the example of Richard Moore, head of the British Secret Intelligence Service. After joining MI6, Moore spent several years in Turkey in the early 1990s. There he learned Turkish and became an active fan of the local football club Beşiktaş. Translated from the language of social life into professional terms, this means that he was recruiting members of Istanbul's elite. At that time, Recep Tayyip Erdoğan, now president of Turkey, was just beginning his political career in Istanbul. Those efforts bore fruit many years later, when Moore became the United Kingdom's ambassador to Turkey and one of Erdoğan's most valuable supporters and influential friends. Surely the mutual acquaintances he had made thirty years earlier helped!

Third, get closer to the real leaders, the key figures in a company or community. Your task here is simple: learn from them and discover something new and useful for yourself. After all, to reach their present positions, these people took action and accomplished something, and the results suggest that their actions were right. They most likely understand how the real world and their own organizations work better than others do, and better than you do. They also have access to certain resources. By drawing closer to influential people, you gain the opportunity to learn from them and emulate their approaches and behavior. Ideally, you should strive to make them your mentors in the hope that, one day, you will inherit their connections and status.

The famous Soviet intelligence officer Iosif Grigulevich (1913-1988), using the name Teodoro B. Castro, passed himself off as the illegitimate son of a wealthy Costa Rican coffee producer. In Rome, he founded an import-export company and established extensive personal contacts with local businesspeople, especially those with ties to the Catholic Church. Over time, he managed to become a close friend and business partner of the former president of Costa Rica. As a result, in 1951, "Teodoro B. Castro" was appointed Costa Rican ambassador to Italy and the Vatican, making him a key insider among the upper ranks of the Catholic Church and Italian political circles.

Riding the "Social Elevators"

When it comes to your position in society as a whole, raising your social status is like riding social elevators. You need to determine which elevators are going up and find a way to jump aboard.

Viewed strategically, the choices we periodically have to make in life can put us on different career trajectories, some higher and others lower. Some paths look more promising from the outset. Being born to wealthy and influential parents clearly makes life and a career easier, just as scoring top marks on Russia's Unified State Exam (EGE) or the American SAT (Scholastic Assessment Test) improves your chances of admission to Moscow State University or Stanford. Even if we cannot control our starting point, however, the later trajectory of our lives depends on the choices we make. These trajectories do not necessarily lead upward. Many intelligent children of famous parents ultimately become failures, alcoholics, or drug addicts. A good education, the right choice of profession, industry and employer, successful friends, and a supportive spouse can radically alter your life's trajectory. At any moment, you may find yourself beside an "elevator" offering short-term and long-term opportunities, and that elevator can carry your life to a higher level. You simply need to seek out these elevators and be ready to jump aboard. Since almost every opportunity for further advancement depends on knowing the right people, your life strategy and networking strategy must operate as a single whole. Your task is to create an "elevator of opportunities" for yourself, as a professional intelligence officer would.

Education - Your "Ticket" Upward

The most obvious, simple, and accessible social elevator is education. Almost any educational program will improve your skills and status. The best programs can become reliable pathways into elite circles. Our intelligence careers included studying in four university programs; the last culminated in a master's degree from the John F. Kennedy School of Government at prestigious Harvard University in the United States. It was a difficult climb to the "summit," considering that we began in an unfamiliar country without even a high school diploma. We had to study at night and work during the day.

The greatest benefit of education, however, is not the new knowledge you gain but the new people you meet. The world of education, including universities, short-term business programs, and professional courses, is society's most open and egalitarian environment. It allows people from different social strata to interact without the social barriers that are inevitably present in everyday life. If you want to build connections beyond your daily circle, you will find no better place.

In addition, people come to study because they are either seeking new knowledge and opportunities or are dissatisfied with themselves and their current work. This means that they are searching for themselves and are open to everything new, including new friends. If you want to fill your network with interesting and successful people, educational institutions have no equal in this respect.

Join High-Status Groups

What else can put you on a better trajectory and raise your status? Membership in high-status groups, of course. Joining private clubs and attending exclusive events are obvious but often expensive choices. We therefore suggest considering other options: professional societies, nonprofit organizations, alumni associations, cigar and wine clubs, sports teams, political parties, and groups that advocate on issues of public importance. Explore opportunities to volunteer, attend think-tank presentations, and help sponsor public events, amateur theater productions, and exhibitions.

Klaus Schwab is a wonderful example of someone who made a name and a career for himself by organizing prestigious events. At age thirty-three, while a professor at a Swiss university, he created a discussion forum now known as the World Economic Forum. Schwab can be considered a peerless networker. He skillfully used his organization to establish connections with presidents and kings around the world. His most remarkable achievement, however, is that he created a networking machine that did this work for him! As the saying goes, hats off to such talent!

While building your career and climbing the corporate ladder, you naturally spend most of your time with people in or around your business and profession. If you want to establish yourself in the upper strata of society, however, you will need to make a special effort to expand your network and build connections outside your professional sphere.

There are periods of rapid career growth, but never rule out stagnation or even decline. Your status in professional circles is closely tied to the status of your employer. If luck turns against you, your colleagues may lose interest in you. The same will happen when you retire. Relationships that developed in other areas of life, however, will most likely endure. After all, they are based less on business and professional interests than on all sorts of other interests.

As an additional benefit, a broad range of connections in different spheres will allow you to become a "connector" for your friends and facilitate exchanges in "relationship currency." Most importantly, you will have something money cannot buy: an interesting and varied life!

Enter the Public Arena

Another way to raise your social status is to become publicly known. You do not have to win the Nobel Prize in Literature or become a senator to hold a prominent place in society. Even average bloggers can amass up to several hundred thousand followers. If you have interesting content, you do not need to own media platforms such as CNN, RT, or Al Jazeera to share it with the rest of the world. Almost any degree of fame gives you leverage in the public arena and raises your status. Once again, content plays a key role! If you have the opportunity, write, make a film, or publish interesting material. If you do not feel capable of creating good content yourself, become a content aggregator or manage a content platform under your own name. The range of possible content is truly limitless, from cooking lessons to international politics.

Any attempt to do something interesting or unusual will inevitably attract other people to you, whether you are crossing a continent by bicycle or writing a book about growing tomatoes. You will get a double benefit. First, you will find yourself in a new community of cycling enthusiasts or tomato growers, giving you access to new, large international networks for communication.

Second, you will find yourself at the center of attention. People will be drawn to you out of natural curiosity. You have probably heard of the attraction that the mysterious world of "bohemia" holds for influential politicians and businesspeople. The best-known example is Mata Hari, a Dutch exotic dancer in Paris during World War I. She was accused of using her connections with high-ranking French officials to spy for Germany. Although Mata Hari's effectiveness as a spy was greatly exaggerated, the attraction that rich and influential people felt toward the young performer was very real.

To underscore the advantage creative people enjoy in networking, we would like to cite the example of the Soviet deep-cover intelligence officer and talented architect Yuri Shevchenko. During the Cold War, he worked in Europe under cover as an artist and achieved remarkable results. For his many years of work, he received Russia's highest state honor, the title of Hero of Russia. For a time, he alone supplied as much as one-third of all classified NATO documents in Soviet intelligence's possession.

Help Solve Important Problems

By responding to a recognized public need as an organizer or participant in projects important to a city or company, you earn recognition and respect. Few people would have noticed the Swedish schoolgirl and activist Greta Thunberg if they had not been concerned about the environment.

Every city and every organization has problems that provoke debate and attract public interest. Issues involving politics, the environment, education, security, or urban life always need people willing to help solve them.

The media "feed" on these problems. Get involved and take part in public discussions, and you will immediately become a valuable guest for reporters and interviewers, whose job is to search constantly for content to fill the 24-hour news cycle. If you also move from words to action, say, by organizing a cleanup of a local river, you might even become a media hero.

Just make sure that the issue you take on does not provoke negative emotions, is neither petty nor merely short-term, and does not make others feel that you are advancing only your personal interests. Many bloggers with millions of followers have made a great deal of money promoting their "brand" and all kinds of products. Yet they often inspire more envy than admiration among their followers. Your public image should help you win friends and allies, not merely attract envious spectators.

If you feel unable to do this alone, find people or organizations working on issues that matter to you and offer to help. We are confident that your investment of time and energy will soon earn you a place among their leaders.

"A Rolling Stone Gathers No Moss!"

One unusual way of raising your status that we discovered as intelligence officers is to remain constantly "in motion," both by traveling and by continually changing your own life.

The benefits of travel are obvious. Flights, tourist trips, cruises, and hotels in remote places all provide remarkable opportunities to meet interesting people who are also looking for new experiences. The people you meet on such trips tend to be more dynamic, sociable, and successful. These are exactly the kinds of people you need as friends. If you work diligently to make contacts while traveling and then develop those relationships, many of them may well become the next rung on your career ladder. It is not for nothing that people say, "Travel is the only thing you buy that makes you richer." When it comes to new friends and opportunities, you certainly cannot argue with that! During our work, for example, we used vacations to visit wine regions, from France to California, South Africa, and Argentina, and to meet people whose backgrounds interested us. Good wine was an added bonus!

Being in motion, in the sense of being willing to relocate or take risks, is essential to raising your status. Staying in one place for too long creates a safe, comfortable emotional environment that gradually binds, entangles, and constrains you, "freezing" your status in the company or society. After a while, those around you no longer expect anything new or exciting from you, and they stop showing the interest they once did. People come to believe that you are simply who you are and have already reached your "ceiling." Once "public opinion" about you has formed, you inevitably become stuck in that niche. This is precisely why successful managers try not to remain in one place too long: they want to follow an "upward" trajectory.

As intelligence officers, we made successful use of the principle of so-called "country-to-country jumps." Each country's elite has its own hierarchy, an unwritten set of rules for assigning status. Money plays a major role in how elite circles judge where you stand relative to others, but family connections and visibility in the media also matter. As soon as your acquaintances learn about your background, family, education, places where you have worked, and so on, they will immediately place you at a particular level in their minds and treat you accordingly. It is virtually impossible to violate the established order and pretend that you occupy a higher social position. People will not grant you higher status until you accomplish something significant enough to deserve it.

Nevertheless, a person arriving from abroad, from another culture, or sometimes simply from another city can "game the system." Unless it is obvious from appearance and behavior, a newcomer's actual social status is difficult for the new social circle to "decipher." The natural curiosity of the local public gives the newcomer a temporary advantage. By default, people will assign them a higher status, at least until their actual accomplishments show otherwise.

The famous Israeli intelligence officer Eli Cohen (1924-1965) was sent by Mossad in 1960 on a special assignment, posing as a successful Syrian businessman. His objective was to gather intelligence in the circles of high-ranking Syrian politicians and military officers. Under the name Kamel Amin Thaabet, he traveled to Argentina and settled in the Syrian emigrant community. While living in Buenos Aires, he became close to a man who later became president of Syria. Cohen eventually moved to Damascus, where he gained entry to the upper echelons of Syrian society. His "project" for building a network of contacts consisted of hosting parties at his home for senior representatives of Syria's political and business worlds. Eli Cohen could never have gained admittance to the tightly closed circles of the Syrian elite had he not arrived from a distant country.

Chapter 8. Launching Operation "Network"

The time has come for decisive, energetic action! You now know what to do to build your "ecosystem" of contacts and how to do it. As we warned at the very beginning, however, in networking, doing matters more than knowing. It will be both difficult and interesting. The purpose of this chapter is to give you several more practical tips. Some will help you stay confidently on your chosen course; others will once again warn you about possible mistakes.

Find a Coach

Everyone knows how difficult it can be to make yourself face daily problems and disappointments. That is precisely why we turn to fitness coaches, dietitians, and family counselors. The same applies to networking. Your plans may look perfect in theory, but when the time comes to meet someone after a long workday, you may feel too tired to carry them out. Moreover, the people you meet may not yet be very interested in you. As a result, you may feel disappointed and demotivated.

That is why, at such moments, you need someone nearby whom you can turn to for help. You need a coach who can listen to your problems, support you, and suggest a solution. More important still, you need someone who can ask difficult questions and make you think. Your coach should serve as a kind of mirror in which you can see yourself as you really are. This is someone with whom you can discuss and think through your plans and actions. You cannot improve without an honest and objective view of yourself from the outside. Your coach can point out your shortcomings while also motivating you to take further action. Try to find among your friends someone who can give you an honest and unbiased assessment. Not all close friends can do this. Since everyone has their own judgments and "blind spots," having two such coaches is even better. Your long-term goal is to surround yourself with trusted people who can help you advance not only as a networker, but also as a professional and a person. And do not forget your personal "advisory board"!

Choose a Role Model

As social beings, we learn how to behave by imitating others. There is nothing wrong or humiliating about following another person's example in an effort to equal and surpass their achievements. Knowing that we might be watched, with every step recorded and analyzed, we learned to imitate patterns of behavior characteristic of different peoples and cultures. Imagine crossing several borders using different passports. If you do not pay attention to how you behave and what you say, a single careless gesture can betray you as quickly as an incorrect answer to a border guard. A good example is the disastrous mistake made by Michael Fassbender's character in Quentin Tarantino's film "Inglourious Basterds." Posing as a German officer, he "blew his cover" by counting on his fingers in a way a German would not...

Hence our advice: find someone you admire as a networker and leader, and analyze the qualities that make them attractive to others. Observe how they interact with other people and try to emulate their behavior. Better still, try to contact this person, ask for advice, and become friends with them.

Learn to Motivate Yourself

Motivation is a key component of your networking. As intelligence officers, we enjoyed a privileged position: we were driven by a deep understanding of the importance of our work, our patriotic convictions, and our ambitions. We had no shortage of motivation. When we faced difficulties and fatigue, we understood that we could not turn aside and thereby admit our inadequacy as officers and professionals. In ordinary life, anyone can find all kinds of excuses and pretexts. Many people become accustomed to putting everything off "until later." After all, you can always find a reason to give yourself a break. We are not superhuman...

You should regard the effort you spend on networking not as an expense but as an investment. Just as you invest money, here you invest time in a better future and in the new opportunities your network of contacts will later bring. Investing follows the law of compound interest: the more you reinvest, the larger the capital base and the faster the balance in your account grows. The earlier you start investing, the more your savings will have grown by retirement. The same law applies to networking: the earlier you start, the larger your network will be, the faster it will grow, and the larger it will ultimately become. Momentum creates momentum. People already in your network will themselves lead you to new contacts without any special effort on your part. Each new person multiplies your future opportunities, and each increase in social capital raises your status.

Don't Be Afraid of Rejection

Another important communication skill is learning to rein in your fear of rejection. Be prepared to take the blows calmly. Some people definitely will not like you, and some may even show you the door in no uncertain terms. As intelligence officers, we accumulated more than enough such cases over the course of our careers. Making so-called "cold calls" is always unpleasant, but nothing can replace the experience of overcoming fear and awkwardness when communicating with complete strangers; it is priceless. Remember the energetic young men with badges and boxes who once went door to door, rang apartment doorbells, and offered irons, kettles, and God knows what else? Many people refused to speak to them, but some agreed to buy the goods they offered. Experienced salespeople will confirm that precisely this kind of sales experience teaches valuable skills and toughens you. So do not be afraid: unpleasant work involving communication with different sections of the public will also help you overcome your fear of public speaking.

On our very first day at business school, we were given a group exercise that seemed partly amusing and partly like a cruel joke. We had to go to a busy intersection and sell drinking water in plastic cups to pedestrians. In the center of a large city, we looked utterly ridiculous. Yet the public would not have rejected a child selling homemade lemonade in those same plastic cups.

The point is that children have no innate fear of embarrassing themselves; they simply walk up and ask for what they need. Society and its various codes of what is "right" and what is not instill in us a fear of rejection. Different cultures have their own so-called "acceptable forms of behavior." For example, starting a conversation with a stranger on the street may seem perfectly normal in the United States or Italy but will be frowned upon in Finland, Japan, or Russia. Moreover, if people reject your offer, you are unlikely to see them again, so why does it matter? Over time, when you have thousands of contacts and hundreds of rejections behind you, you will develop a thick "elephant skin" and, with it, a philosophical attitude toward life.

"Camouflage" Your Networking Among Other Activities

But where can you find time for all this? There is no "magic" formula. You cannot set aside time for living and separate time for communicating. It simply does not work that way. Networking must become an organic part of your life. In our experience, learning to network is like learning a foreign language or learning to play the violin: to develop the skill, you must practice every day, even if only for a few minutes.

Whatever you are doing and wherever you are going, think about whom you can talk to and whom you can meet. Conversely, plan your activities, including education, sports, walks, and vacations, with your networking goals constantly in mind. Take the schedule of events and tasks you are planning for the next month and add networking work day by day; this will help you distribute your time and effort more effectively. If you have divided people into priority groups, use your free time, for example, while waiting for a doctor's appointment or a flight at the airport, to call or email someone or to like and comment on posts. Compile an annual list of birthdays and, according to your priorities, schedule visits and dinners and buy gifts so that all of this fits around your other activities and trips. Review the schedule of interesting events and conferences and optimize it for your networking strategy. Invite new acquaintances to exercise or travel with you while developing those relationships. Hold parties for several friends at once, and invite new acquaintances with whom you want to strengthen your ties. The more proactively you behave and the better you plan and organize, the more effectively you will learn to use your time.

Keep a Diary

For an intelligence officer, keeping a diary would probably be the most foolish idea imaginable: to counterintelligence, it would be a priceless trove of information, completely exposing all the secret work.

Yet a diary performs a vital function, especially during the initial stages of any new and complex undertaking: it is excellent at organizing your thoughts and serves as a virtual interlocutor. In addition, by preserving a record of events and thoughts, a diary used for networking allows you to return to earlier meetings and conversations, "record" facts and emotions, and view what happened from the vantage point of accumulated experience.

From a purely practical standpoint, your diary will become a repository of information about your contacts. Together with the calendar-planner included in every annual organizer, it will help you, as we just discussed, fit networking more effectively into the flow of your daily activities. Your regular entries will also provide invaluable material for analyzing your progress!

Periodic Analysis of Your Network

Stage 1

Looking at the map of your contact network, ask yourself: How well suited is your network to the challenges you face? What are your goals in life for the next year or two? Where do you see yourself? How closely do the people in your network align with the goals you have set? Can they help you achieve what you are planning? Which of your contacts do you spend most of your time with? Is this the most effective use of your time? Do you have relationship problems with anyone in your network? Mark the most important people and assess how your contacts with them are developing. Mark the relationships you want to repair.

Stage 2

Take a pen of another color, say red, and mark on your network map the changes you would like to make. Who can help you, and how can you meet them? Where are the people you would like to learn from? Can any of your current contacts introduce you to them? Mark those who could become your mentors. Write down the people you want to meet. See whether any of your existing contacts can introduce you to them. Then take another sheet of paper and write an action plan for the next month.

Stage 3

At least once a month, set aside a quiet hour in the evening to review your personal contact network. Assess how successfully you implemented your plan and what you could improve. Add new acquaintances to the map. Analyze changes in your relationships over the past month. Repeat Stages 1 and 2. This should become a habit and a necessary routine. In six months, you will see for yourself what remarkable results you have achieved in networking!

Respect Others' Personal Space and Time

For most of this book, we have been introducing you to networking and teaching you the skills and strategies needed to succeed. Like any honest teacher, however, we believe it is important to pass on not only knowledge but also the wisdom we have gained from our own mistakes. Our discussion will therefore not be complete until we address the ethics of networking and the problems we ourselves have encountered.

Networking is, first and foremost, a social activity. Beyond simply striving to be a good person, you must respect others and consider how your actions affect other people and the wider world.

Everyone has met people who pester others and refuse to take no for an answer. Do not become one of them. For a professional, such behavior is a sure path to failure. As we have already said, intelligence officers should be "gardeners," not "hunters," in their relationships with other people. If you want relationships to flourish, you must respect other people's personal space and privacy. Intruding into someone's private life or disregarding the boundaries they set is one of the fastest ways to destroy a budding relationship at its roots. Worse still, word of your aggressive tactics will damage future relationships with other people, often before you ever meet them and without your knowing it.

You must also remember that people have only a limited amount of attention to devote to you. Everyone has things going on in their life, and in most cases you will not be their first priority. Do not demand too much of your friends and acquaintances. In addition to having limited time for you, people have limits to their emotional and empathic capacity. There is a reason people say, "A problem shared is a problem halved" (or, to put it in more Russian terms, "Two heads are better than one"). Still, do not expect others to bend over backward for you. There is a fine line between positive support and emotional dependence. Respect the fact that, however much most people may want to help you, they have lives of their own and a limited capacity to care for others.

Friendship can be built, but it cannot be imposed. What is most obvious in romantic relationships applies equally to all interpersonal relationships. Once you see a clear sign that someone does not want to deepen their relationship with you, you must accept the situation and stop trying to change it. As we have already said, you should always have a virtual GoPro camera with you. If these people eventually change their minds, they will come back to you themselves. As a good networker, you should always have a "Plan B," so that no rejection, however painful, becomes an insurmountable obstacle to achieving your goals in life.

We would also like to remind you of the risks that close relationships entail. It is no secret that the most terrible and bloody feuds often occur within families and among the closest friends. The reason is quite simple: when we trust someone, we give that person an opportunity to hurt us where we are most vulnerable. You must always be mindful of how you treat the people closest to you. As intelligence officers, we staked our lives on trusting our Service, our colleagues, and our compatriots. The same tension exists, although at a less potentially fatal level, in every close relationship, whether in business or in personal life. You must do everything possible to retain other people's trust, and you must never use information shared with you "in confidence" to hurt or threaten others.

The final point we would like to mention is the importance of respecting boundaries. Networking is not a game, and people are not tools. We cannot give you a complete, exhaustive list of what is and is not ethical. You must determine that for yourself and establish your own boundaries. Most importantly, you must be able to recognize those boundaries before you reach them. If you fail to do so, you will find yourself on a slippery slope, justifying one action after another as they become increasingly unethical and self-destructive.

Don't Try to Manipulate People

As you master networking techniques and gain experience communicating productively with different people, you may be tempted to use your abilities to make others do something that benefits you against their will. Even if you are pursuing the noblest and most righteous goals, there is always a risk of falling into an ethical trap, especially if you use unacceptable means to achieve them. Many highly effective methods of managing interpersonal relationships may prove less than ethical. Con artists, abusive partners, and criminals often resort to such methods. If you ever feel the urge to use them, suppress it. Besides being unethical, these methods will most likely cause you serious harm in the long run. Your objective, after all, is to build healthy, productive relationships strategically, not to twist people's arms through emotional pressure or coercion.

In practice, such methods are most often based on false or misleading information in which an ordinary person would find nothing suspicious. In the field of information warfare, these methods are known as "social engineering." Leaders of religious cults, pimps, and con artists use various psychological techniques to control their victims. Without going into the details of these prohibited methods, we will point out one characteristic feature: they exploit the stronger emotional response to receiving help than to experiencing pain, even when both come from the same person.

An abusive partner will first inflict harm and emotional anguish on a victim, then reverse course and offer emotional support. Although this emotional "roller coaster" is a source of suffering and misery, it is a highly effective means of creating emotional dependence and making victims want to prove themselves worthy of their abuser. Under no circumstances should you use such methods, or anything similar, to grow closer to people, much less to control them. Instead of exploiting other people's weaknesses, you should focus on their strengths and build close relationships on a positive foundation. Fear may seem like an effective tool, but its effects are short-lived, and it is ultimately a double-edged sword. In intelligence, fear is the very last resort, and one that can be used only once.

Learn to Forget and Forgive

Achieving your goals is important, but not at any cost. You are building your network to improve the quality of your life, not to prove something to someone else. Nor are you doing it simply to expand your network without end or chase some high-profile contact. As professionals, we have often had occasion to reflect on the purpose of networking. Early in our careers, an official came onto our radar whose capabilities and connections quite literally made our mouths water as intelligence officers. We were only "one handshake" away from this person and, as the rules required, prepared a detailed plan for approaching the official for our leadership at the "Center." The response from the "Center" quite literally shocked us. "Leave him alone," the coded cable read. "You are not yet capable of handling a contact like that." Disappointed and upset, we tried to argue, but to no avail. A year or two later, during a clandestine meeting with one of our operational supervisors, we raised the subject of the official again. "Look, guys," came the reply. "We track an enormous number of contacts maintained by your colleagues around the world. We see what works and what does not. We keep statistics on contacts, including yours. Even if you had managed to establish this contact, you would not have been able to maintain it. You would have had nothing to offer a person at such a high level. The contact would have withered, and losing it would have eliminated any chance of approaching this person again in the future, once you were ready." In other words, do not bite off more than you can chew...

The wealth of practical experience accumulated by intelligence-service leaders once again reinforces the argument we have developed throughout this book: networking and social status go hand in hand; they are two sides of the same coin. There is no point in accumulating ever more contacts that neither enhance your social standing nor help solve your problems. In the end, the quality of your relationships is what matters. To produce the results your friends and colleagues expect from you, you will have to work at becoming increasingly knowledgeable, resourceful, and appealing.

In short, the better you become, the better your network becomes. As your value to others grows and new opportunities open up to you, increasingly high-level contacts will come within your reach.

Networking, like politics, is "the art of the possible." It concerns what we are realistically capable of doing. Not everything is within our control. Some relationships will inevitably prove difficult. Some people you consider friends will suddenly begin scheming and spreading gossip about you. Someone will ask to borrow money from you and then stop answering your calls. Unfortunately, humanity is still far from perfect. All we can do is keep our own promises and hope that others do the same. Human relationships will always remain something of a lottery - sometimes we win, and sometimes we lose. But take note: if the same relationship problems arise again and again, that is a reason to look at yourself and ask whether you may have overlooked something...

Sometimes it is best simply to turn the page and move on. Preserving the health of your ecosystem of contacts is far more important than trying to retain particular relationships at any cost, even the most prestigious ones. No one is indispensable. Do not take slights too personally. Do not cling to troubled relationships. Never seek revenge. Do not waste your time and energy - keep moving forward. In strategic networking, good spirits and motivation are your greatest assets. After all, you have more important things to do!

Conclusion. Networking as Self-Improvement

Perhaps the most important lesson our intelligence work taught us was to be proactive. We had no choice but to take control of everything happening around us, especially our contacts. Our path toward the objective meant that every move and every contact had to have a purpose and a plan. As intelligence officers, we automatically became the designers and builders of our human environment. Time after time, step by step, we surrounded ourselves with the people we needed. Our objectives in working with people could vary, but in every case we used a proven set of methods.

Just like spies, you can manage your own human "ecosystem." You have the power to make networking a purposeful and effective process. There is a clear, proven set of rules for raising your social status. There is, however, one important condition: you must be prepared to make a serious effort.

People care about the quality of the things around them. Where we live and what we keep in our homes matter to us. Our human environment, however, has an incomparably greater effect on the quality of our lives than material possessions do. Our success, and even our happiness, depend on how well the people around us suit our character and our goals. Yet we are often willing to accept our environment just as it happened to develop, without trying to eliminate the problems built into it. We allow the people around us to determine our fate, forgetting that we have tools of our own for influencing that environment.

There is, however, one exception! We take great care in choosing our life partners - our spouses. We dream of a person with a particular appearance and character; we search for that person, build a relationship, love them, and care for them over many years. We would not agree to choose a spouse blindly, because we understand how profoundly that person will affect our life. Even with all this care, however, we sometimes make the wrong choice.

What about all the other people around us? Distracted by everyday minutiae, we forget our closest friends. We allow dissatisfaction and conflict to damage relationships and sometimes eat away at us from within. We are too lazy to make new acquaintances, and we miss excellent opportunities.

We wrote this book to convince our readers that each of them can use strategic networking to build a human "ecosystem" organized according to their own standards. We urge readers to abandon a passive, haphazard, and unconsidered approach to their contacts. Instead, we strongly encourage everyone to invest time and effort in a network composed of people they value and respect.

Try applying the strategic networking techniques described in this book. There is nothing mystical or inexplicable about strengthening your social status: it is a clear, step-by-step process available not only to exceptional networking geniuses such as David Rockefeller, Klaus Schwab, or Richard Sorge. We have traveled this path ourselves several times, and we are confident that you can do it too.

As a proactive, strategic networker, you will have the privilege of deliberately filling your environment with people whom you need and enjoy. You will be able to choose the people with whom you would most like to work, making your life more interesting or your business more successful. As you develop contacts and your "ecosystem" expands, you will gain the freedom to "disconnect" from those who bring you nothing but pain and problems. With a large network of contacts, you will always have a choice about whom to interact with and whom to avoid. You will certainly never have to be bored and alone.

Although networking requires persistence and patience, it invariably results in new discoveries: meeting wonderful new people whom you otherwise would never have encountered; discovering diverse and exciting dimensions of life that you would never have explored without making new friends; and, finally, discovering opportunities that would have remained inaccessible if you had not taken that difficult first step yourself.

Networking, of course, is not an end in itself but merely a means to an end. It is a mechanism for continually improving your life and yourself. As you surround yourself with more and more people who are, in many respects, better, more knowledgeable, more experienced, more skillful, and wealthier than you, you also gain opportunities to learn and enrich yourself in every sense of the word. The better and more accomplished the people around you are, the better you become and the better your life becomes. In conclusion, allow us to paraphrase the medieval Persian philosopher and poet Omar Khayyam and remind you that your life will be shaped by the company you keep.
